## 1. System Overview & Spatial Hierarchy

The Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) platform optimizes spatial water management and climate‑adaptation interventions across complex deltaic systems. It operates across a two‑tier spatial and temporal hierarchy, decoupling macro‑level hydrological allocation from micro‑level physical execution.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                       MACRO-AGENT (Sub-Basin Scale)                     │
│  • Spatial Bounds: 200 x 300 Grid (1 km² Resolution)                   │
│  • Time Horizon: 5–10 Years (Seasonal Steps)                            │
│  • Core Task: Continuous Inter-Basin Water Allocation Vectors (b_k)     │
└────────────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────┘
                                     │
                  Passes Target Boundary Vectors (b_k)
                                     │
                                     ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                      MICRO-AGENT (Reach / Plot Scale)                   │
│  • Spatial Bounds: 100 x 100 Grid (100 m² Resolution)                   │
│  • Time Horizon: 1 Year (Monthly Steps)                                 │
│  • Core Task: Discrete Physical Placement & Local Governance Actions    │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The resolution parameters — 1 km² macro, 100 m² micro — are adopted provisionally and require stress-testing during Phase B to verify boundary condition transmission fidelity across the three-order-of-magnitude resolution jump. Intermediate resolution tiers should be evaluated if boundary artefacts at the macro-micro interface prove significant.

This distinction is maintained throughout to avoid conflating satellite-derivable physical measurements with governance variables that require human fieldwork to measure reliably.

## 2. Directed HydroECA: The Optimisation Target

### 2.1 Formal Definition

The RL agent's primary optimisation target — Hydrological Equivalent Connected Area (HydroECA) — is defined on a directed, asymmetric graph to reflect the anisotropic physics of water flow. The directed formulation corrects the isotropic assumption of the standard ecological ECA metric, which would produce systematically incorrect connectivity estimates when applied to a hydrological domain.

$$\text{HydroECA} = \sum_{i} \sum_{j} a_i \cdot a_j \cdot p_{ij}^{*}$$

where:

- $a_i$, $a_j$ are the storage or attenuation capacities of nodes $i$ and $j$, measured in volumetric units normalised to area equivalents;
- $p_{ij}^{*}$ is the **directed transmission probability** of water flow from node $i$ to node $j$ along the least-resistance path through the network.

Critically, $p_{ij}^{*} \neq p_{ji}^{*}$ in general: transmission probability is asymmetric, reflecting hydraulic gradient, channel geometry, and resistance along the directed path.

### 2.2 Directed Transmission Probability

$$p_{ij}^{*} = e^{-\theta \cdot R_{ij}^{*}}$$

where $R_{ij}^{*}$ is the least-resistance directed path cost from $i$ to $j$ across the resistance surface, and $\theta$ is a scale parameter calibrated to BWDB observed flood routing data.

The directed path cost is computed on a directed graph in which edge weights encode hydraulic resistance — a function of slope, channel geometry, land cover, soil permeability, and embankment presence — with directional asymmetry imposed by slope and hydraulic gradient. Backwater effects under flood conditions can reverse the effective connectivity of reaches that are unidirectional under normal flow; this is not an edge case in the GBM delta but a routine monsoon-season condition affecting large portions of the floodplain.

### 2.3 Seasonal Regime Switching

HydroECA is computed on two season-specific directed graphs:

$$\text{HydroECA}_{\text{total}} = \lambda_m \cdot \text{HydroECA}_{\text{monsoon}} + \lambda_d \cdot \text{HydroECA}_{\text{dry}}$$

where $\lambda_m + \lambda_d = 1$ are programme-specified weights reflecting the relative management priority assigned to flood attenuation versus dry-season water availability. These are policy parameters set by Bangladesh NbS programme managers, not RL agent hyperparameters.

Transitional periods (April–May and October–November) require interpolated resistance parameterisations between the two seasonal directed graphs. Channel 0 of the state tensor is updated by the Graph Analysis Engine at each timestep as the directed resistance graph evolves through satellite-derived channel updates and intervention outcomes.

### 2.4 HydroECA Sensitivity Requirements

Sensitivity analysis of HydroECA to the scale parameter $\theta$ and to resistance layer values must be conducted during Phase B before the metric is used as a primary performance indicator or reward signal. Particular attention should be paid to the stability of HydroECA rankings across seasonal resistance graph variants — if the relative ordering of intervention sites reverses between monsoon and dry-season parameterisations, the seasonal weighting policy parameters $\lambda_m$ and $\lambda_d$ will require careful calibration before agent training begins.

## 3. Action Space

### 3.1 Macro-Agent Action Space

The macro-agent operates over a continuous action space of inter-basin water allocation vectors. At each seasonal timestep, the macro-agent outputs:

$$\mathbf{b} = \{b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_K\} \in [0,1]^K, \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} b_k = 1$$

where $b_k$ is the fractional capital budget allocation to sub-basin $k$ in the current seasonal step, and $K$ is the number of active sub-basins in the planning domain. These boundary vectors are passed to the relevant micro-agent instance for the sub-basin and constrain its action budget for the period.

The macro-agent also outputs governance investment allocation vectors $\mathbf{g} = \{g_1, \ldots, g_K\}$, specifying the fraction of community engagement resources directed to each sub-basin. This enables the GFI exploration mechanism (Section 5.2) to operate at basin scale as well as cell scale.

### 3.2 Micro-Agent Action Space

The micro-agent operates over a discrete action space of physical placement and governance actions at 100 m² reach or plot scale. The action set $\mathcal{A}$ at each monthly timestep includes:

**Physical intervention actions:**
- $a_{\text{riparian}}(u, \mathbf{s})$: Plant riparian buffer at cell $u$ with species composition vector $\mathbf{s}$
- $a_{\text{wetland}}(u)$: Initiate wetland restoration at cell $u$
- $a_{\text{floodplain}}(u)$: Reconnect floodplain at cell $u$
- $a_{\text{mangrove}}(u, \mathbf{s})$: Plant mangrove at coastal cell $u$ with species composition $\mathbf{s}$
- $a_{\text{retention}}(u)$: Construct agro-ecological water-retention system at cell $u$
- $a_{\text{null}}$: No physical intervention this timestep

**Governance actions:**
- $a_{\text{engage}}(u)$: Initiate Union Parishad community engagement at cell $u$
- $a_{\text{tenure}}(u)$: Commission land tenure clarification process at cell $u$
- $a_{\text{cooperative}}(u)$: Establish or strengthen cooperative governance structure at cell $u$

Physical intervention actions are masked when Channel 13 (σ_morph) ≥ 0.85 or Channel 8 (GFI) ≤ 0.25, per the Stage 2 Red Alert protocol (Section 6.1). Governance actions remain available during Red Alert to support future de-escalation.

Monsoon-season action masking additionally suppresses physical placement actions during active flood phase (June–October), restricting the micro-agent to governance actions and planning during this period.

## 4. Reward Function

### 4.1 Composite Reward Structure

The reward function integrates six components weighted by programme-specified hyperparameters:

$$R_t = w_1 \Delta\text{HydroECA}_t + w_2 R_{\text{eff},t} + w_3 R_{\text{mat},t} - w_4 C_t + w_5 B_{\text{GFI},t} + w_6 R_{\text{cobenefit},t} - w_7 P_{\text{sal},t}$$

**Component 1 — HydroECA gain** ($w_1 \Delta\text{HydroECA}_t$): The primary reward signal. The change in directed HydroECA resulting from action $a_t$ at state $S_t$, computed on the updated directed resistance graph incorporating the intervention. Seasonal weights $\lambda_m$, $\lambda_d$ apply internally. This component dominates the reward signal; all other components are secondary.

**Component 2 — Hydraulic efficiency** ($w_2 R_{\text{eff},t}$): Penalises geometrically inefficient interventions that fail to connect high-storage nodes. Elongated, narrow riparian buffers that do not bridge larger floodplain units receive lower efficiency scores than configurations that connect high-storage wetland nodes. Computed from the graph-theoretic bridging coefficient of the intervention within the sub-basin network.

**Component 3 — Hydrological effectiveness maturity** ($w_3 R_{\text{mat},t}$): Tracks soil permeability recovery, riparian canopy establishment, and sediment stabilisation over time. Replaces the Miyawaki ecological maturity component with a hydrologically parameterised index. Increases monotonically with intervention age up to an asymptotic maximum, discounting immature interventions whose hydraulic function has not yet developed.

**Component 4 — Implementation cost** ($-w_4 C_t$): Bangladesh-specific implementation cost incorporating community engagement time, land negotiation costs, species procurement, planting labour, and monsoon-season access constraints. Calibrated from pilot district cost accounting during Phase A.

**Component 5 — GFI exploration bonus** ($w_5 B_{\text{GFI},t}$): Incentivises governance investment in high-HydroECA-value but currently low-GFI cells, counteracting the self-reinforcing spatial concentration that would otherwise result from GFI endogeneity:

$$B_{\text{GFI},t} = \beta_{\text{GFI}} \cdot \mathbb{1}[\text{GFI}(u) < \tau_{\text{GFI}}] \cdot \Delta\text{HydroECA}^{*}(S_t, a_t)$$

where $\Delta\text{HydroECA}^{*}(S_t, a_t)$ is the counterfactual HydroECA gain that would result from action $a_t$ if GFI were at its maximum value, $\beta_{\text{GFI}}$ is the exploration intensity hyperparameter, and $\tau_{\text{GFI}}$ is the low-GFI threshold below which the bonus activates. Setting $\beta_{\text{GFI}} = 0$ recovers behaviour without exploration incentive. $\beta_{\text{GFI}}$ and $\tau_{\text{GFI}}$ are calibrated against programme capacity constraints during Phase B.

**Component 6 — Co-benefit bonus** ($w_6 R_{\text{cobenefit},t}$): Rewards interventions generating measurable agricultural productivity gains alongside hydrological benefits, reflecting the NbS framework's emphasis on multi-sectoral returns. Derived from predicted yield-volatility reduction in the intervention cell and adjacent agricultural cells, estimated from Channel 4 (Agricultural Land Classification) and SARE DSI/CRSI projections.

**Component 7 — Salinity intrusion penalty** ($-w_7 P_{\text{sal},t}$): Penalises interventions that increase or fail to reduce coastal salinity penetration. Rewards interventions that reduce salinity exposure in agricultural zones, derived from Channel 5 (Salinity Intrusion Index) projections under the proposed intervention.

### 4.2 Reward Component Weights

Default weight initialisation for Phase B training:

| Component | Default Weight | Notes |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| $w_1$ (HydroECA gain) | 0.50 | Primary signal; dominates |
| $w_2$ (Hydraulic efficiency) | 0.10 | Secondary geometric penalty |
| $w_3$ (Maturity) | 0.10 | Discounts immature interventions |
| $w_4$ (Cost) | 0.10 | Normalised to pilot district cost baseline |
| $w_5$ (GFI exploration) | 0.10 | Calibrate $\beta_{\text{GFI}}$, $\tau_{\text{GFI}}$ separately |
| $w_6$ (Co-benefit) | 0.05 | Agricultural productivity signal |
| $w_7$ (Salinity penalty) | 0.05 | Coastal zone activation only |

Weights are programme hyperparameters subject to calibration during Phase B and review at each phase gate. They are not learned by the agent; they encode programme priorities set by Bangladesh NbS management.

## 5. Governance Feasibility Index

### 5.1 GFI Definition and Update Protocol

The Governance Feasibility Index (GFI) encodes the current estimated probability of successful intervention implementation at a given spatial cell $u$. It is stored in Channel 8 of the state tensor as a continuous value in $[0,1]$.

The GFI integrates six sub-indices, each updated at specified intervals:

| Sub-index | Update Frequency | Data Source |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Land tenure clarity and dispute status | Quarterly | Bangladesh Land Records; UP dispute registry |
| Union Parishad engagement level | Monthly | Field officer reports |
| Community cooperative participation | Monthly | DAE cooperative records |
| Ministerial coordination status | Quarterly | NbS coordination unit records |
| Seasonal access conditions | Monthly | SARE FEI (monsoon access constraint) |
| Adjacent cell NbS adoption rate | Monthly | Implementation tracking database |

The GFI for cell $u$ at time $t$ is computed as a weighted composite of sub-indices:

$$\text{GFI}(u,t) = \sum_{k=1}^{6} \phi_k \cdot I_k(u,t), \quad \sum_{k=1}^{6} \phi_k = 1$$

Sub-index weights $\phi_k$ are calibrated from Phase A pilot district data on the predictive validity of each sub-index for implementation success. Initial weights are set equal pending calibration.

### 5.2 GFI Endogeneity and the Exploration Mechanism

The RL agent's recommendations will tend to concentrate community engagement resources in already-accessible cells, raising those cells' GFI further and leaving high-HydroECA low-GFI cells without governance investment. This is a structural feedback endogeneity problem that must be explicitly managed rather than assumed away.

The GFI exploration bonus ($B_{\text{GFI},t}$, Section 4.1 Component 5) addresses this at the reward function level. A second safeguard operates at the HITL governance layer: quarterly pipeline audits flag any sub-basin in which no low-GFI high-HydroECA cells have received governance investment in the preceding 90 days. Such cells trigger a Yellow Alert on the GFI dimension, prompting a 72-hour review of community engagement sequencing.

The exploration mechanism produces recommendations that pair near-term physical implementation in accessible areas with medium-term governance investment in high-value inaccessible areas — generating a sequenced pipeline that is actionable today and strategically positioned for subsequent phases.

## 6. HITL Risk Escalation and Governance Safeguards

### 6.1 Two-Stage Risk Escalation Matrix

Operational risks are managed through continuous monitoring of Channel 13 (σ_morph) and Channel 8 (GFI):

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  STAGE 1: YELLOW ALERT (Advisory)                                    │
│  Trigger: σ_morph ∈ [0.65, 0.85)  OR  GFI ∈ (0.25, 0.40]           │
│  Action:  3× w_morph penalty injection; 72-hr field audit alert      │
└──────────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┘
                                   │ Breach severity escalation
                                   ▼
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  STAGE 2: RED ALERT (Emergency Fail-Safe)                            │
│  Trigger: σ_morph ≥ 0.85  OR  GFI ≤ 0.25                            │
│  Action:  Physical action masking P(s, a_physical) = 0; 24–48hr HITL │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Stage 2 automated action masking zeros all physical action vectors directed at cell $u$ within the MCTS selection layer. The tree search engine automatically backpropagates a topology re-route, setting node capacity $\alpha_u = 0$ and shifting capital budget allocations to secondary candidate nodes within the sub-basin.

### 6.2 De-Escalation Gate

A cell is unmasked and returned to the MCTS action pool only after satisfying two conditions simultaneously:

$$\sigma_{\text{morph}}(u) < 0.65 \quad \text{AND} \quad \text{GFI}(u) > 0.40 \quad \text{for} \geq 2 \text{ consecutive monthly timesteps}$$

This dual-trigger de-escalation prevents premature return to action in geomorphologically active cells and ensures that community governance endorsement is required alongside technical clearance before physical interventions resume.

### 6.3 Manual Override Logging

All human-in-the-loop overrides are cryptographically signed and immutably logged, providing an auditable record of every departure from RL-generated recommendations. Each override log entry records:

- Triggering channel value(s) at time of override
- Operator identity and authorisation level
- Override type (advisory clearance / permanent abandonment)
- Justification text (minimum 50 words)
- Timestamp and sub-basin identifier

This log constitutes the accountability record for parliamentary tabling (per the NbS monitoring framework, Part 1 Section 7.5) and for climate-finance reporting under results-based instruments.

## 7. Transition Model

### 7.1 Hydrological Transition Dynamics

The transition model simulates how physical interventions change the landscape's hydrological state between timesteps. It employs a reduced-order neural emulator trained on outputs from the national hydrological model (Part 3, Section 3.3), enabling evaluation within the RL training loop at computational cost tractable for MCTS rollouts.

Core transition dynamics for each intervention type follow logistic growth with lateral propagation:

$$H(u, t+1) = H(u, t) + r_H \cdot H(u, t) \cdot \left(1 - \frac{H(u,t)}{K_H}\right) + D_H \nabla^2 H(u,t) + \epsilon_t$$

where $H(u,t)$ is the hydrological effectiveness index at cell $u$ and time $t$, $r_H$ is the intervention-type-specific establishment rate, $K_H$ is the asymptotic effectiveness ceiling, $D_H$ is a lateral propagation coefficient encoding connectivity spillover to adjacent cells, and $\epsilon_t$ is a stochastic disturbance term.

The reduced-order neural emulator is retrained quarterly on updated hydrological model outputs to prevent drift from real-world transition dynamics.

### 7.2 Morphodynamic Disturbance Module

The GBM delta undergoes substantial morphodynamic change on timescales of months to years. Riverbank erosion and char land formation operate well within the planning horizon; a static resistance layer would become misleading faster than the transition model can compensate through ecological succession alone.

A stochastic morphodynamic disturbance module updates Channels 2, 3, 5, and 13 at each timestep by sampling from a spatially structured disturbance distribution:

$$\Delta \sigma_{\text{morph}}(u, t) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_z, \sigma_z^2)$$

where $\mu_z$ and $\sigma_z^2$ are zone-specific mean and variance of morphodynamic change rate, parameterised from BWDB historical erosion and accretion time series stratified by geomorphological zone:

| Zone | Characteristic Process | Update Priority |
|------|----------------------|----------------|
| Active channel margin | Rapid bank erosion / char accretion | High |
| Char land | Accretion and submergence cycles | High |
| Stable floodplain | Slow lateral migration | Medium |
| Coastal fringe | Storm surge erosion; mangrove accretion | High |
| Upland terrace | Minimal morphodynamic activity | Low |

The disturbance distribution is updated annually as new BWDB monitoring data become available. This is a reduced-form stochastic emulator, not a full morphodynamic model; the tradeoff between physical accuracy and computational tractability within the MCTS rollout budget requires explicit validation during Phase B.

### 7.3 Monsoon-Aware Temporal Structure

The transition model distinguishes three operational modes, each with distinct dynamics:

**Monsoon season (June–October):** Flood dynamics dominate. Physical intervention is masked. HydroECA$_{\text{monsoon}}$ governs reward computation. SARE FEI provides near-continuous flood extent updates. Morphodynamic disturbance rates are elevated.

**Dry season (November–May):** Physical planting and restoration are feasible. HydroECA$_{\text{dry}}$ governs reward computation. SARE optical indices (Sentinel-2) are at maximum availability. Community engagement, governance investment, and field measurement campaigns concentrate in this window.

**Transitional periods (April–May, October–November):** Mixed conditions. Interpolated resistance parameterisations. Partial action masking. Elevated uncertainty in SARE index estimates reflected in EnKF ensemble variance.

A monsoon-mode binary flag is appended to the state tensor observation at each timestep, enabling the agent to condition its policy on the current data quality and action availability regime.

## 8. Compute Architecture

### 8.1 Asynchronous Parallel Vectorised Monte Carlo Tree Search (APV-MCTS)

The decision engine implements APV-MCTS with the following configuration:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  APV-MCTS CONFIGURATION                                             │
│                                                                     │
│  Macro-Agent                                                        │
│  • PUCT exploration constant:  c_puct = 1.25                        │
│  • Rollout budget:             1,600 simulations per decision step   │
│  • Virtual loss injection:     v = 3                                │
│                                                                     │
│  Micro-Agent                                                        │
│  • PUCT exploration constant:  c_puct = 2.50                        │
│  • Rollout budget:             800 simulations per decision step     │
│  • Virtual loss injection:     v = 8                                │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The PUCT selection criterion at each node is:

$$a^* = \arg\max_a \left[ Q(s,a) + c_{\text{puct}} \cdot \pi_\theta(a|s) \cdot \frac{\sqrt{N(s)}}{1 + N(s,a)} \right]$$

where $Q(s,a)$ is the mean value estimate for action $a$ at state $s$, $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ is the policy network prior, $N(s)$ is the visit count at the parent node, and $N(s,a)$ is the visit count for the child node reached by action $a$.

The higher $c_{\text{puct}}$ for the micro-agent reflects the denser and more locally variable action space at plot scale, requiring greater exploration relative to exploitation. Virtual loss injection prevents thread collision in the parallel tree traversal and is set higher for the micro-agent to counteract action-space concentration.

### 8.3 EnKF Data Assimilation Pipeline

State tensor channels 0–7 and 12–13 are continuously updated via Ensemble Kalman Filter executing $N_{\text{ens}} = 100$ parallel state ensemble members.

The Kalman Gain is localised using a Gaspari-Cohn correlation function with cutoff radius $r = 15$ km to eliminate spurious long-distance covariances across hydrologically isolated sub-basins:

$$\mathbf{K}_{t,\text{loc}} = \left(\mathbf{P}_t^f \mathbf{H}^T\right) \odot \mathbf{C}_{\text{GC}}(r) \cdot \left(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}_t^f\mathbf{H}^T + \mathbf{R}_t\right)^{-1}$$

Dynamic variance inflation $\lambda_t \geq 1.0$ is applied to the analysed anomaly matrix before writing the mean state tensor to VRAM, preventing ensemble collapse under sustained low-innovation periods.

Community observations enter the assimilation pipeline via the block-structured observation error covariance matrix:

$$\mathbf{R}_t = \text{blkdiag}\left(\mathbf{R}_t^{\text{instrument}}, \mathbf{R}_t^{\text{community}}\right)$$

where $\mathbf{R}_t^{\text{community}}$ has larger diagonal entries calibrated from Phase D pilot data, ensuring community observations are appropriately down-weighted relative to instrument telemetry in proportion to their uncertainty. When a community report is substantially down-weighted due to conflict with satellite evidence, the discrepancy is flagged to the field team for ground-truth investigation rather than silently absorbed.

## 9. Network Architecture

### 9.1 Policy and Value Networks

Both macro and micro agents employ residual convolutional neural networks operating over the spatial state tensor. The base architecture:

```
Input: S_t ∈ ℝᴴ×ᵂ×¹⁴
  │
  ▼
Conv2d(14 → 128, kernel=3, padding=1) + BatchNorm + ReLU
  │
  ▼
[Residual Block × 10]  (128 channels throughout)
  │
  ├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  ▼                                                      ▼
Policy Head                                         Value Head
Conv2d(128 → 2, kernel=1)                      Conv2d(128 → 1, kernel=1)
Flatten → FC(|A|) → Softmax                    Flatten → FC(256) → FC(1) → tanh
π_θ(a|s) ∈ Δ(|A|)                             V_θ(s) ∈ [-1, 1]
```

Residual blocks follow the standard pre-activation formulation (He et al., 2016). The policy head output dimension $|A|$ differs between macro (continuous allocation) and micro (discrete placement) agents; the micro-agent policy head uses a spatial softmax over the action grid rather than a flat action vector.

### 9.2 Training Protocol

Both agents are trained via self-play against the hydrological transition model, following the AlphaZero training loop:

1. **Self-play data generation:** APV-MCTS generates trajectories using current policy and value networks. State tensors, MCTS action distributions, and episode outcomes are stored in a replay buffer (capacity: 500,000 positions).

2. **Network update:** Policy and value networks are updated by minimising the combined loss:

$$\mathcal{L} = (z - V_\theta(s))^2 - \boldsymbol{\pi}^T \log \pi_\theta(s) + c\|\theta\|^2$$

where $z$ is the episode outcome (discounted cumulative HydroECA gain), $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ is the MCTS action distribution, and $c$ is an L2 regularisation coefficient.

3. **Evaluation:** Updated networks are evaluated against the current best network over 400 games. Networks that win ≥ 55% of evaluation games replace the current best.

4. **Phase-gated update:** Network updates during active deployment are gated by HITL review when the proposed update would substantially alter recommendations in high-GFI, high-HydroECA cells already under implementation.

## 10. Phase D Validation Protocol

### 10.1 Experimental Design

Phase D evaluates the full platform across a 24-month field deployment in 16 coastal Union Parishads: 8 treatment UPs receiving RL-guided NbS interventions and 8 control UPs receiving standard district masterplan interventions, separated by a 5 km buffer zone to minimise spatial spillover.

### 10.2 Synthetic Control and Difference-in-Differences

For each treatment UP $i$, a synthetic control $i^*$ is constructed via optimal donor weighting $W^*$ minimising pre-intervention outcome divergence (parallel trends verified at $p < 0.01$). Treatment effects are estimated dynamically:

$$\hat{\tau}_{it} = \left(Y_{it} - Y_{i,t_0}\right) - \left(Y_{i^*t} - Y_{i^*,t_0}\right)$$

where $Y_{it}$ is the outcome indicator (HydroECA, SARE salinity index, agricultural yield) for UP $i$ at time $t$, and $t_0$ is the intervention start date. This framework resolves the counterfactual attribution requirement identified in Part 2 Section 7.4: without it, the adaptive management loop cannot reliably update the transition model from monitoring data.

### 10.3 Phase D Exit Gates

Full spatial rollout (Phase E) requires clearance of four quantitative gates over the 24-month testing window:

| Gate | Metric | Threshold | Statistical Criterion |
|------|--------|-----------|----------------------|
| 1 | HydroECA gain in treatment vs. synthetic control | ≥ 18 units | p < 0.01 |
| 2 | RMSE: predicted vs. observed HydroECA | < 12 | — |
| 3 | GFI exploration pipeline health | Zero cells in governance deadlock > 9 months | — |
| 4 | Agricultural soil salinity reduction (dry-season peak) | ≥ 2.5 dS m⁻¹ | Statistically significant |

Gate 3 directly tests the GFI exploration mechanism (Section 5.2): if $\beta_{\text{GFI}}$ is correctly calibrated, no high-HydroECA low-GFI cell should remain without governance investment for more than 9 months. Gate 4 tests the salinity intrusion penalty component of the reward function under real-world conditions.

Gates must be cleared simultaneously; sequential clearance with subsequent regression on any gate metric triggers a formal programme review and Phase D extension before Phase E proceeds.

## 12. Limitations

**Governance variable observability.** Channels 8–11 depend on field survey and administrative data streams that are less frequent, less spatially granular, and less objective than satellite-derived channels. GFI values in particular will lag real-world governance changes. The quarterly update protocol is a practical compromise; faster update would improve agent performance but exceed current field capacity.

**Neural emulator fidelity.** The reduced-order neural emulator of the hydrological transition model trades physical accuracy for computational tractability within MCTS rollout budgets. Emulator drift from real-world dynamics will accumulate between quarterly retraining cycles; the magnitude of acceptable drift requires explicit specification and monitoring.

**Attribution under multi-intervention landscapes.** In mature programme phases where many sub-basins carry simultaneous NbS interventions, attributing observed HydroECA changes to specific actions becomes progressively more difficult. The synthetic control framework (Section 10.2) addresses this during Phase D but requires adaptation for programme-wide attribution in Phase E and beyond.

**Community acceptance of algorithmic recommendations.** The GFI exploration mechanism will sometimes recommend governance investment in areas that community members and field teams consider unpromising. Communicating the rationale — high latent HydroECA value — to non-technical audiences is a co-design challenge that must be addressed in field team training and community interface development, not assumed to resolve itself through interface design alone.

**Political economy of institutional creation.** The platform's value depends on sustained inter-ministerial cooperation, a functioning NbS coordination unit, and Bangladesh Bank data-sharing agreements. None of these are guaranteed. The platform should be designed to deliver value through its hydrological and community governance functions independently of any single institutional dependency.

## 13. Conclusion

The HRL platform specified here provides the computational architecture that translates the national NbS governance framework (Part 1) and RL-NbS synthesis (Part 2) into an operational planning and adaptive management system, supported by the digital-twin infrastructure (Part 3) and SARE telemetry backbone (Part 4).

Version 0.3 resolves the principal mathematical and architectural gaps identified in prior review: the directed HydroECA metric correctly represents the anisotropic physics of delta hydrology; the GFI exploration mechanism prevents governance-driven spatial concentration; the morphodynamic disturbance module propagates realistic landscape change uncertainty into the transition model; and the Phase D exit gates provide quantitative clearance criteria for progression to national deployment.

Five outstanding requirements — directed resistance layer calibration, GFI weight calibration, macro-micro boundary validation, HydroECA sensitivity analysis, and neural emulator training data — constitute the critical path for Phase B. None require conceptual departures from the architecture specified here; all are empirical and engineering tasks with defined deliverables.

The platform is designed to generate value progressively from Phase A pilot deployment, with each phase contributing data that improves subsequent phase performance. The 25-year programme horizon is not a constraint on early impact; it is the timescale over which the compounding benefits of spatially optimised, governance-aware, community-executed NbS intervention are expected to fully materialise across Bangladesh's delta system.
