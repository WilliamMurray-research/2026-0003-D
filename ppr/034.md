## ABSTRACT

The Satellite-Enabled Agricultural Risk Engine (SARE) is the primary data backbone of Bangladesh's national Nature-Based Solutions (NbS) digital-twin architecture. It transforms multi-sensor satellite telemetry — principally Sentinel-1 SAR, Sentinel-2 multispectral, and auxiliary datasets — into seven high-frequency, spatially explicit risk indices that map directly onto hydrological and agricultural channels of the reinforcement learning (RL) state tensor. SARE resolves the largest technical gap in the RL-NbS synthesis: the availability of continuously updated, spatially explicit hydrological resistance data at the resolution required to keep the 14-channel state tensor current between field measurement cycles.

This paper specifies SARE's sensor architecture, index derivation pipelines, uncertainty quantification framework, operational processing schedule, and integration protocols with the Ensemble Kalman Filter (EnKF) assimilation engine and the directed HydroECA graph computation module. Seven primary indices are defined: Flood Extent Index, Soil Moisture Volatility Index, Drought Stress Index, Salinity Intrusion Index, Erosion Hazard Index, Crop Rotation Stability Index, and Canopy Cover Change Index. Each index is specified with its sensor inputs, algorithmic derivation, output resolution and frequency, target state tensor channel, and uncertainty characterisation.

SARE is designed as a national public infrastructure for Bangladesh, with index outputs made available to the Bangladesh Water Development Board, the Department of Agricultural Extension, the banking and insurance sectors, and Union Parishad governance systems. A financial-sector output layer generates district-level agricultural credit risk scores derived from SARE index composites, enabling the financial feedback loop through which HydroECA gains reduce bank provisioning requirements and NbS borrowing costs.

**Keywords:** satellite telemetry, agricultural risk, SAR, Sentinel, Bangladesh, hydrological indices, digital twin, NbS, HydroECA, remote sensing, EnKF

## 1. INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 The Data Gap in RL-Optimised NbS Planning

The reinforcement learning optimisation engine developed in Part 2 of this series trains an agent to maximise directed Hydrological Equivalent Connected Area (HydroECA) across Bangladesh's delta landscape through sequential NbS intervention placement. The RL environment requires a 14-channel state tensor — a spatially explicit, continuously updated representation of the landscape's hydrological, agricultural, ecological, socio-political, and morphodynamic state — to be operationally useful.

Populating this tensor presents a fundamental data challenge. The required variables span multiple physical domains (hydrology, soil science, ecology, geomorphology), require spatial resolution of 10–100 metres across a national domain, and must be updated at frequencies ranging from near-daily (flood extent, salinity) to monthly (canopy cover, morphodynamic change). No existing national monitoring network in Bangladesh provides this combination of coverage, resolution, and frequency. The Bangladesh Water Development Board's gauge network provides high-quality point measurements of river discharge and water level but has no spatial interpolation infrastructure for floodplain conditions. Field campaigns provide spatially explicit measurements but at infrequent intervals and with significant deployment costs.

Satellite remote sensing resolves this challenge. The current generation of operational satellite systems — particularly the European Space Agency's Sentinel constellation — provides near-daily to sub-weekly repeat coverage of Bangladesh at spatial resolutions of 10–20 metres, in both synthetic aperture radar (SAR) and multispectral optical bands, at no direct data acquisition cost. The technical challenge is not data availability but algorithmic processing: translating multi-sensor, multi-temporal satellite imagery into the physically interpretable, uncertainty-quantified risk indices required by the RL state tensor and the EnKF assimilation engine.

SARE is the pipeline that performs this translation.

### 1.2 SARE's Role in the System Architecture

Within the digital-twin architecture specified in Part 3, SARE occupies the interface between the satellite data stream and the computational layer. Its outputs feed:

1. **The 14-channel state tensor** (Channels 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 12, 13 directly; indirect influence on Channels 0 and 7 through the directed HydroECA graph and GFI update protocols).
2. **The directed resistance layer** used in HydroECA computation, which requires continuous updates to land cover, soil permeability, and inundation depth.
3. **The EnKF background forecast** as the primary observation vector at each assimilation timestep.
4. **The financial-sector credit risk layer**, which aggregates SARE indices into district-level agricultural risk scores consumed by the banking and insurance sectors.

SARE does not generate governance or socio-political variables. Channels 8 (GFI), 9 (Land Ownership), and 10 (Labour Availability) are populated from field survey and administrative data streams outside SARE's scope. Channel 11 (Groundwater Table Depth) is populated from the BWDB piezometer network. These distinctions are maintained throughout to avoid conflating satellite-derivable physical measurements with governance variables that require human fieldwork to measure reliably.

## 2. SENSOR ARCHITECTURE AND DATA ACQUISITION

### 2.1 Primary Sensor Platforms

SARE draws on four primary satellite platforms, selected for operational availability, spatial resolution, temporal frequency, and complementarity across cloud-affected and cloud-free conditions.

**Sentinel-1 SAR (ESA, C-band, dual polarisation VV+VH)**

Sentinel-1 provides cloud-penetrating synthetic aperture radar imagery with a 6-day repeat cycle at 10 m resolution in Interferometric Wide (IW) swath mode. SAR backscatter is sensitive to surface roughness, dielectric properties, and water content, making it the primary sensor for flood extent mapping, soil moisture estimation, and morphodynamic change detection under all weather conditions, including the heavy cloud cover and precipitation that characterise Bangladesh's monsoon season. This is SARE's most operationally critical platform: optical sensors become blind during monsoon precisely when hydrological risk is highest.

C-band SAR limitations include: signal attenuation under dense vegetation canopy (relevant for mangrove and riparian buffer monitoring); confusion between open water and specular-reflecting built surfaces (relevant in peri-urban areas); and sensitivity to wind-roughening of water surfaces that can obscure flood extent boundaries. These limitations are addressed through the multi-sensor fusion protocols specified in Section 3.

**Sentinel-2 Multispectral (ESA, 13 bands, 10–60 m resolution)**

Sentinel-2 provides 10–20 m multispectral imagery at a 5-day repeat cycle (combining Sentinel-2A and 2B). Its 13 spectral bands spanning visible, near-infrared (NIR), and shortwave infrared (SWIR) wavelengths support vegetation index computation, crop classification, water quality estimation, and soil property inference. Sentinel-2 is the primary sensor for agricultural land classification, crop phase identification, canopy cover monitoring, and salinity intrusion proxy estimation. Its 5-day repeat enables detection of rapid agricultural stress events (within-season drought, post-flood waterlogging) that coarser-temporal sensors would miss.

Cloud cover is a critical constraint. In Bangladesh, cloud cover exceeds 80% for much of the June–September monsoon period, rendering Sentinel-2 data largely unavailable when hydrological risk is greatest. SARE's cloud-gap strategy — Sentinel-1 SAR primacy during monsoon, Sentinel-2 during dry season, fusion algorithms during transitional periods — is specified in Section 2.3.

**MODIS / VIIRS (NASA, daily, 250–500 m resolution)**

MODIS (Terra/Aqua) and VIIRS (Suomi-NPP/NOAA-20) provide daily coverage at 250–500 m resolution across Bangladesh. Their coarse resolution precludes field-level spatial discrimination but their daily frequency makes them valuable for rapid flood onset detection, drought stress monitoring, and fire detection. SARE uses MODIS/VIIRS primarily as a gap-filling and anomaly-detection layer: rapid changes in MODIS flood extent or vegetation stress trigger priority reprocessing of the next available Sentinel acquisition.

**Auxiliary Datasets**

- **SRTM / Copernicus DEM (30 m):** Digital elevation model providing topographic context for flood routing, slope computation, and drainage basin delineation. Updated infrequently; treated as quasi-static infrastructure within SARE.
- **BWDB Gauge Network:** River water level and discharge data from approximately 600 stations across Bangladesh. These in-situ measurements are not satellite-derived but are ingested by SARE as calibration anchors for SAR-derived inundation depth estimates and as direct inputs to the hydrological background forecast model.
- **Bangladesh Meteorological Department (BMD) Station Network:** Daily rainfall, temperature, and humidity data for drought stress index computation and monsoon phase classification.
- **NASA GPM IMERG:** Global Precipitation Measurement satellite product providing 0.1° / 30-minute precipitation estimates. Supplementary to BMD station data, providing spatially continuous coverage in data-sparse areas.

### 2.3 Cloud-Gap and Monsoon-Season Strategy

Bangladesh's monsoon season (June–October) imposes persistent cloud cover that can render Sentinel-2 optical imagery unavailable for weeks at a time across much of the country. This is a structural challenge for any optical-primary remote sensing system operating in Bangladesh. SARE's architecture treats this as a first-order design constraint rather than an edge case.

The cloud-gap strategy operates in three modes:

**Dry-season mode (November–May):** Sentinel-2 optical data is available at near-full frequency. Sentinel-1 SAR provides complementary structural and surface moisture information. SARE operates at maximum index precision and temporal density.

**Monsoon-season mode (June–October):** Sentinel-2 is predominantly clouded. Sentinel-1 SAR becomes the primary data source for all indices where SAR-based derivation is feasible (flood extent, soil moisture, erosion hazard, morphodynamic change). Indices requiring optical inputs (salinity proxy, crop classification, canopy cover) are maintained through: (i) persistence from the last valid Sentinel-2 observation, with increasing uncertainty inflation applied linearly with days since last valid observation; (ii) MODIS/VIIRS coarse-resolution updates for anomaly detection; and (iii) Sentinel-1 coherence-based proxies for vegetation change where optical methods are unavailable.

**Transitional mode (April–May, October–November):** Mixed cloud conditions. Scene-by-scene cloud masking determines which algorithm path applies. Uncertainty estimates explicitly reflect transitional-period data quality degradation.

This three-mode structure is reflected in the monsoon-aware temporal architecture of the RL environment (Part 2 v0.2, Section 3.5.1): the RL agent receives a monsoon-mode flag in the state tensor that signals the current data quality regime, allowing it to appropriately discount index-derived channels during periods of reduced observability.

## 3. SARE INDEX SPECIFICATIONS

SARE produces seven primary risk indices. Each is specified below with its sensor inputs, algorithmic derivation, output resolution and frequency, target state tensor channel, uncertainty characterisation, and seasonal mode behaviour.

### 3.1 Index 1: Flood Extent Index (FEI)

**Target channel:** Ch 3 (Seasonal Inundation Depth)
**Update frequency:** 6-day (Sentinel-1 primary); daily (MODIS gap-fill)
**Output:** Inundation fraction and estimated water depth per 10 m cell; aggregated to 100 m and 1 km for tensor ingest

**Derivation:**

Flood extent is detected using threshold-based change detection applied to Sentinel-1 VV-polarisation backscatter. Open water surfaces produce specular backscatter at C-band incidence angles, returning very low backscatter values ($\sigma^0 < -15\,\text{dB}$ in VV). A temporally adaptive threshold is computed per scene using Otsu's method applied to the backscatter histogram within each 1 km² processing tile, separating the water and non-water populations.

The binary flood/no-flood classification is converted to inundation depth estimates by fusing with the Copernicus DEM. For each inundated cell, a local hydrological connectivity analysis identifies the nearest non-inundated river gauge cell; DEM-derived depth-area relationships calibrated against BWDB gauge water levels at the calibration stations provide cell-level depth estimates.

**Monsoon-season note:** FEI is SARE's most reliable monsoon-season product. SAR cloud penetration means FEI maintains near-nominal update frequency throughout the wet season. It is the primary state tensor update during monsoon periods.

**Known limitations:** Dense emergent vegetation (particularly water hyacinth) suppresses backscatter and can mask shallow inundation beneath canopy. Urban specular reflectors can produce false water detections. Depth estimates degrade with distance from gauge calibration points. Uncertainty maps accompanying each FEI product flag cells exceeding distance thresholds from calibration anchors.

### 3.2 Index 2: Soil Moisture Volatility Index (SMVI)

**Target channel:** Ch 1 (Soil Permeability & Infiltration Rate)
**Update frequency:** 6-day (Sentinel-1); rolling 90-day volatility window
**Output:** Coefficient of variation of surface soil moisture (0–5 cm) per 10 m cell; 90-day rolling window

**Derivation:**

Sentinel-1 VV and VH backscatter are combined to retrieve surface soil moisture fraction using a Water Cloud Model (WCM) that accounts for vegetation attenuation. The WCM is parameterised with vegetation water content estimated from concurrent Sentinel-2 NDVI where available, or from the most recent valid NDVI observation during clouded periods with temporal decay weighting.

Soil moisture retrievals at each Sentinel-1 overpass are assembled into a 90-day rolling time series per cell. The SMVI is computed as the coefficient of variation (CV) of this time series:

$$\text{SMVI}_u = \frac{\sigma_{\text{SM},u}^{(90)}}{\mu_{\text{SM},u}^{(90)}}$$

where $\sigma_{\text{SM},u}^{(90)}$ and $\mu_{\text{SM},u}^{(90)}$ are the standard deviation and mean of soil moisture retrievals for cell $u$ over the preceding 90 days.

High SMVI indicates highly variable surface moisture — a proxy for low infiltration capacity and high runoff generation, corresponding to low soil permeability. Low SMVI indicates stable moisture conditions consistent with higher infiltration. The SMVI is inverted and rescaled to produce the soil permeability proxy fed to Channel 1.

**Physical interpretation for NbS:** NbS riparian and wetland interventions increase soil organic matter and root macroporosity, reducing SMVI over time as infiltration capacity improves. Improvement in Channel 1 values following NbS implementation is a measurable outcome indicator.

---

### 3.3 Index 3: Drought Stress Index (DSI)

**Target channel:** Ch 12 (Hydro-meteorological Flood Risk Index — drought component)
**Update frequency:** Monthly (primary); 16-day (MODIS NDVI anomaly for interim updates)
**Output:** Standardised precipitation-evapotranspiration deficit index [−3, +3] per 0.1° grid cell, downscaled to 1 km

**Derivation:**

The DSI is computed as a modified Standardised Precipitation-Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) at 1-month and 3-month accumulation periods. Precipitation inputs are drawn from the BMD station network interpolated to a 0.1° grid, supplemented by NASA GPM IMERG for spatial gap-filling. Reference evapotranspiration is computed using the FAO Penman-Monteith equation with BMD temperature, humidity, and wind speed inputs.

$$\text{SPEI}_{u,t} = \Phi^{-1}\left(F\left(D_{u,t}\right)\right)$$

where $D_{u,t} = P_{u,t} - \text{ET}_{0,u,t}$ is the monthly water balance deficit at cell $u$ and time $t$, $F(\cdot)$ is the log-logistic cumulative distribution function fit to the historical deficit series, and $\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)$ is the standard normal inverse CDF.

Interim drought signals between monthly SPEI computations are provided by MODIS NDVI anomalies: departures from the 2000–2025 monthly mean NDVI climatology at 250 m resolution, aggregated to 1 km and scaled to the SPEI range for consistency.

Channel 12 combines the DSI (drought component) and the FEI-derived flood probability (flood component) into a composite hydro-meteorological risk score. The combination weights are season-dependent: the drought component dominates during dry season; the flood probability component dominates during monsoon.

### 3.4 Index 4: Salinity Intrusion Index (SII)

**Target channel:** Ch 5 (Soil & Surface Water Salinity)
**Update frequency:** 10-day (Sentinel-2 primary); 6-day SAR proxy during monsoon
**Output:** Estimated surface water electrical conductivity (EC, dS/m) and soil exchangeable sodium percentage (ESP) proxy per 10 m cell

**Derivation:**

Surface water salinity is estimated from Sentinel-2 imagery using a spectral index sensitive to dissolved salt concentration in water bodies. The Normalised Difference Salinity Index (NDSI) is computed from SWIR and NIR bands:

$$\text{NDSI} = \frac{\rho_{\text{SWIR1}} - \rho_{\text{NIR}}}{\rho_{\text{SWIR1}} + \rho_{\text{NIR}}}$$

NDSI is calibrated against in-situ EC measurements from the BWDB saline water intrusion monitoring network (approximately 150 stations in coastal districts) using a locally weighted regression model updated quarterly. The regression model maps NDSI to EC (dS/m) with spatially varying coefficients reflecting the influence of suspended sediment concentration on spectral salinity signals in turbid delta waters.

Soil salinity is estimated separately using a SWIR-based soil salinity index applied to non-inundated agricultural cells, calibrated against Bangladesh Soil Resources Development Institute (SRDI) field measurements.

**Monsoon-season proxy:** When Sentinel-2 is unavailable, SII is maintained using Sentinel-1 coherence change as a proxy for salinity-induced soil surface change and a persistence model that propagates the last valid SII forward using known salinity intrusion dynamics (upstream penetration rate approximately 1–3 km/day during dry season, retreat during monsoon).

**Significance for reward function:** Channel 5 feeds the salinity intrusion penalty term in the RL reward function. The SII directly measures the variable the reward function is penalising, creating a direct link between satellite observation and agent incentive structure.

### 3.5 Index 5: Erosion Hazard Index (EHI)

**Target channel:** Ch 13 (Morphodynamic Volatility Rate, $\sigma^\text{morph}$)
**Update frequency:** Monthly (Sentinel-1 coherence); 6-monthly (SAR change detection for channel migration)
**Output:** Estimated annual bank erosion / accretion rate (m/year) per 50 m cell; normalised morphodynamic volatility index [0, 1]

**Derivation:**

The EHI is the most technically demanding SARE index, requiring detection of sub-metre lateral channel migration and bank erosion at monthly timescales across the GBM delta's morphodynamically active zones.

**SAR coherence change detection:** Sentinel-1 interferometric coherence — the phase correlation between pairs of co-registered SAR images — degrades rapidly over areas experiencing surface change (erosion, deposition, vegetation change, inundation). Multi-temporal coherence matrices computed from consecutive Sentinel-1 image pairs (12-day baseline) identify areas of active surface change. Coherence loss in riverbank zones signals active erosion or deposition.

**Multi-temporal bank position mapping:** Bank positions are delineated from each Sentinel-1 flood extent product using morphological edge detection applied to the water/land boundary. Bank position time series over 6-month windows are used to compute lateral migration rates per bank segment:

$$\sigma_u^{\text{morph}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} \left| x_{u,k} - x_{u,k-1} \right|$$

where $x_{u,k}$ is the bank position at time $k$ and $N$ is the number of observations in the window. This rate is expressed in m/year and normalised to the [0, 1] range using the 99th percentile of observed rates across Bangladesh's historically active erosion zones as the normalisation anchor.

**Geomorphological zone classification:** Bangladesh's delta is stratified into five geomorphological zones — active channel, recent char, stable floodplain, tidal flat, and coastal fringe — each with characteristic morphodynamic behaviour. The EHI uncertainty model and the HITL escalation thresholds (Yellow Alert: $\sigma^\text{morph} \geq 0.65$; Red Alert: $\sigma^\text{morph} \geq 0.85$) are calibrated within each zone, reflecting the substantially higher natural variability of active channel and char zones relative to stable floodplain areas.

### 3.6 Index 6: Crop Rotation Stability Index (CRSI)

**Target channel:** Ch 4 (Agricultural Land Classification & Crop Phase)
**Update frequency:** Seasonal (3 full crop seasons: Kharif-I, Kharif-II, Rabi)
**Output:** Crop type classification map per 10 m cell; crop phase (vegetative / reproductive / harvest / fallow) per 10 m cell; rotation stability score [0, 1] per cell aggregated over preceding 3 years

**Derivation:**

Bangladesh's agricultural calendar comprises three main cropping seasons: Kharif-I (March–June, Aus paddy and vegetables), Kharif-II (July–November, Aman paddy — the main crop), and Rabi (December–March, Boro paddy and winter crops). Accurate within-season crop mapping requires multi-temporal Sentinel-2 time series that capture phenological progression.

**Crop classification:** A Random Forest classifier trained on Sentinel-2 multi-temporal NDVI, Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI), Land Surface Water Index (LSWI), and the red-edge bands (B5, B6, B7) — which are particularly sensitive to chlorophyll content and plant structure — produces a per-cell crop type classification at each seasonal timestep. Training data are drawn from: (i) the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics agricultural census ground truth; (ii) GPS-tagged field survey polygons collected in Phase A of the research programme; (iii) expert-labelled samples from high-resolution commercial imagery.

**Crop phase tracking:** Within each season, the temporal NDVI trajectory is fit to a double-logistic growth model. The phase parameters (green-up date, peak date, senescence date, harvest date) are extracted and used to assign cells to crop phase categories. Phase tracking enables the RL agent to avoid recommending riparian interventions that would disturb standing crops.

**Rotation stability score:** The CRSI is computed as the consistency of crop type classification across the preceding three years at each cell. Stable rotation (aman paddy → boro paddy → fallow, repeated consistently) scores 1.0. Erratic or interrupted rotation — indicative of flood damage, drought stress, salinity intrusion, or tenure insecurity — scores toward 0. Low CRSI values are a multi-causal risk signal that the RL agent should investigate via correlated channels (FEI for flooding, SII for salinity, GFI for tenure).

### 3.7 Index 7: Canopy Cover Change Index (CCCI)

**Target channel:** Ch 6 (Wetland & Mangrove Canopy Cover)
**Update frequency:** Monthly
**Output:** Canopy cover fraction [0–100%] per 10 m cell; monthly change rate; cumulative change since NbS intervention date (where applicable)

**Derivation:**

Canopy cover fraction is estimated from Sentinel-2 using the fractional vegetation cover model:

$$f_c = \left(\frac{\text{NDVI} - \text{NDVI}_\text{soil}}{\text{NDVI}_\text{veg} - \text{NDVI}_\text{soil}}\right)^2$$

where $\text{NDVI}_\text{soil}$ and $\text{NDVI}_\text{veg}$ are bare soil and dense vegetation NDVI endmembers estimated from the tails of the district-level NDVI histogram each month. This model is calibrated against drone LiDAR canopy height model measurements at NbS pilot sites.

**NbS outcome tracking:** The CCCI serves a dual function in the system. As a static land cover descriptor, it characterises the current state of wetland and mangrove cover for Channel 6 at each timestep. As a dynamic outcome tracker, it measures the canopy establishment response following NbS riparian planting and wetland restoration. The RL transition model's hydrological effectiveness index (Part 2 v0.2, Section 3.4) is partially calibrated against CCCI trajectories observed at pilot sites: the rate of canopy closure following planting determines how rapidly the hydrological benefits of a riparian intervention materialise, which in turn determines the transition model's temporal decay parameters.

**Monsoon-season:** Monthly canopy cover estimates are maintained through the monsoon using Sentinel-1 VH backscatter as a vegetation structure proxy, calibrated against Sentinel-2-derived $f_c$ estimates from the most recent cloud-free observations. VH backscatter is sensitive to vegetation volume scattering and provides a coarser but cloud-penetrating canopy density signal.

## 4. UNCERTAINTY QUANTIFICATION FRAMEWORK

### 4.1 Per-Index Uncertainty Characterisation

Each SARE index is delivered with a spatially explicit uncertainty map. The uncertainty characterisation framework follows a three-source structure:

**Sensor uncertainty ($u_\text{sensor}$):** Radiometric calibration error, thermal noise, and geometric co-registration uncertainty in the satellite data. For Sentinel-1, radiometric calibration accuracy is $\pm 0.7\,\text{dB}$, translating to soil moisture retrieval uncertainty of approximately $\pm 0.03\,\text{m}^3/\text{m}^3$. For Sentinel-2, top-of-atmosphere reflectance uncertainty is $\pm 3\%$ after atmospheric correction.

**Algorithm uncertainty ($u_\text{algo}$):** Uncertainty arising from the retrieval algorithm's model assumptions and calibration data limitations. For regression-based indices (SII, SMVI), this is estimated from cross-validation residuals on held-out calibration data. For classification-based indices (CRSI), this is the posterior classification uncertainty from the Random Forest probability output.

**Observational gap uncertainty ($u_\text{gap}$):** Uncertainty introduced by persistence modelling during cloud cover or SAR gap periods. This component increases linearly with days since the last valid observation:

$$u_{\text{gap},u,t} = u_{\text{gap},0} + \gamma \cdot \Delta t_u$$

where $\Delta t_u$ is the days since last valid observation for cell $u$, $u_{\text{gap},0}$ is the baseline gap uncertainty, and $\gamma$ is the temporal decay rate calibrated per index from observed index autocorrelation. During monsoon-season optical gaps, $u_\text{gap}$ can dominate the total uncertainty for optically-derived indices.

**Total uncertainty:** The three components are combined assuming independence:

$$u_\text{total} = \sqrt{u_\text{sensor}^2 + u_\text{algo}^2 + u_\text{gap}^2}$$

### 4.2 Uncertainty Propagation into the EnKF

Total per-index uncertainty maps are used to populate the observation error covariance matrix $\mathbf{R}_t$ of the EnKF assimilation engine (Part 3, Section 4.3). The diagonal elements of the SARE sub-block of $\mathbf{R}_t$ are set to $u_{\text{total},u,t}^2$ for each cell $u$ at each timestep $t$. Off-diagonal elements within the SARE sub-block are set using the Gaspari-Cohn localisation function with a 15 km cutoff, consistent with the EnKF spatial localisation scheme.

This means that SARE observations with high monsoon-season gap uncertainty ($u_\text{gap}$ dominated) are automatically down-weighted in the EnKF analysis relative to gauge and field observations with lower uncertainty. The assimilation framework does not require manual uncertainty escalation during clouded periods; it is automatic through the $u_\text{gap}$ mechanism.

### 4.3 Validation Protocol

SARE index validation is conducted against three reference data types:

1. **In-situ instrument records:** BWDB gauge water levels (FEI), BWDB saline water intrusion EC measurements (SII), SRDI soil survey data (SMVI, SII), BMD climate station records (DSI).
2. **Drone LiDAR campaigns:** High-resolution aerial surveys at NbS pilot sites for CCCI and EHI validation.
3. **Community ground-truth reports:** Farmer group flood depth observations, Union Parishad erosion reports, and agricultural extension officer crop assessments — assimilated via the Bayesian community data protocol and used for independent validation of SARE index agreement with on-the-ground conditions.

Validation statistics (RMSE, bias, correlation, skill score relative to climatological baseline) are computed quarterly and reported to the digital-twin operations team. Indices failing to meet minimum skill thresholds trigger algorithm review before continued use in the state tensor.

## 5. INTEGRATION WITH THE EnKF ASSIMILATION ENGINE

### 5.1 Observation Vector Structure

At each EnKF assimilation timestep, SARE delivers an observation vector $\mathbf{y}_t^\text{SARE}$ containing the gridded values of all seven indices at their native resolution, regridded to the state tensor's 100 m (micro-agent) and 1 km (macro-agent) processing grids. The observation vector is structured as:

$$\mathbf{y}_t^\text{SARE} = \left[\text{FEI}_t, \, \text{SMVI}_t, \, \text{DSI}_t, \, \text{SII}_t, \, \text{EHI}_t, \, \text{CRSI}_t, \, \text{CCCI}_t\right]^\top$$

with accompanying uncertainty covariance $\mathbf{R}_t^\text{SARE}$ as specified in Section 4.2.

### 5.2 Assimilation Frequency

The EnKF assimilation schedule is driven by data availability:

| Observation Type | Assimilation Frequency | Primary Channels Updated |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sentinel-1 (FEI, EHI) | 6-day | Ch 3, Ch 13 |
| Sentinel-2 (SII, CRSI, CCCI) | 5-day (cloud-permitting) | Ch 4, Ch 5, Ch 6 |
| SMVI (rolling window update) | 6-day (Sentinel-1 overpass) | Ch 1 |
| DSI (monthly) | Monthly | Ch 12 |
| MODIS/VIIRS anomaly flags | Daily | Triggers priority reprocessing |
| BWDB gauge data | Daily | Background forecast anchor |
| Community observations | As received | All relevant channels, Bayesian weighted |

During monsoon periods when Sentinel-2 is predominantly clouded, SARE shifts to a reduced-channel assimilation mode: FEI and EHI maintain near-nominal frequency; SMVI updates on every Sentinel-1 overpass; optical-derived channels (SII, CRSI, CCCI) transition to persistence mode with inflating uncertainty.

### 5.3 Handling Missing Observations

When a SARE index is unavailable for a given cell at a given timestep (cloud cover, sensor outage, processing failure), the EnKF ensemble is propagated forward using the background forecast model without an observation update for that cell. The ensemble spread naturally increases during unobserved periods, reflecting growing uncertainty — no artificial uncertainty capping is applied. The HITL risk escalation system monitors ensemble spread and triggers a Yellow Alert for any cell where ensemble spread in Channel 13 ($\sigma^\text{morph}$ uncertainty) exceeds the threshold value for 5 consecutive days without a valid SARE observation, ensuring human review of morphodynamic conditions in data-gap situations.

## 6. INTEGRATION WITH DIRECTED HydroECA GRAPH COMPUTATION

### 6.1 Resistance Layer Updates

The directed HydroECA metric (Part 2 v0.2, Section 3.2) is computed on a directed resistance graph whose edge weights encode hydraulic resistance — a function of land cover, soil permeability, slope, channel geometry, embankment presence, and inundation depth. SARE indices provide the dynamic inputs that update this resistance graph between planning cycles.

The resistance value $r_{uv}$ for a directed edge from cell $u$ to adjacent cell $v$ is computed as:

$$r_{uv} = f\left(\text{LC}_{uv}, \, \text{SMVI}_u, \, \text{FEI}_u, \, \text{CCCI}_u, \, \text{EHI}_u, \, \text{Slope}_{uv}, \, \text{Emb}_{uv}\right)$$

where LC is land cover classification (from CRSI), SMVI is the soil permeability proxy, FEI provides inundation depth, CCCI provides riparian vegetation density, EHI provides morphodynamic volatility, Slope is the DEM-derived gradient, and Emb is the embankment presence mask (Channel 2, from infrastructure mapping).

The functional form $f(\cdot)$ is a weighted multiplicative model calibrated against BWDB observed flood routing data in the Phase A resistance calibration exercise (Part 2 v0.2, Priority 1). SARE updates four of the six non-static inputs to this function: FEI, SMVI, CCCI, and EHI. The resistance layer is therefore updated at the frequency of the slowest of these inputs — monthly, driven by CCCI and EHI — with interim updates whenever FEI or SMVI assimilation triggers a significant resistance change.

### 6.2 Seasonal Graph Switching

As specified in Part 2 v0.2 Section 3.2.4, HydroECA is computed on two season-specific directed resistance graphs:

$$G_\text{monsoon} \quad \text{and} \quad G_\text{dry}$$

SARE provides the seasonal switching signal by computing a monsoon phase classifier from FEI, DSI, and BMD precipitation data:

$$\text{Phase}_t = \begin{cases} \text{monsoon} & \text{if FEI}_t > \tau_\text{FEI} \text{ AND } P_{30,t} > \tau_P \\ \text{dry} & \text{if FEI}_t < \tau_\text{FEI} \text{ AND DSI}_t < \tau_\text{DSI} \\ \text{transitional} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where $\tau_\text{FEI}$, $\tau_P$, and $\tau_\text{DSI}$ are calibrated thresholds. The phase classifier outputs are used by the HydroECA computation module to select the appropriate directed resistance graph and by the RL temporal architecture to select the active season mode. This ensures that the resistance graph and the RL timestep structure are always consistent with the current hydrological regime as observed by satellite.

## 7. FINANCIAL-SECTOR OUTPUT LAYER

### 7.1 District-Level Agricultural Credit Risk Scores

Beyond its role in the NbS digital twin, SARE generates a financial-sector output layer: district-level agricultural credit risk scores derived from index composites. These scores operationalise the financial feedback loop specified in Part 3, Section 7, enabling the link between HydroECA gains and bank provisioning reductions.

The Agricultural Credit Risk Score (ACRS) for district $d$ at time $t$ is a weighted composite of SARE indices aggregated to district level:

$$\text{ACRS}_{d,t} = w_\text{F} \cdot \overline{\text{FEI}}_{d,t} + w_\text{S} \cdot \overline{\text{SII}}_{d,t} + w_\text{D} \cdot \overline{\text{DSI}}_{d,t} + w_\text{E} \cdot \overline{\text{EHI}}_{d,t} - w_\text{C} \cdot \overline{\text{CRSI}}_{d,t}$$

where overbars denote district-level aggregation (area-weighted mean), the risk weights $(w_\text{F}, w_\text{S}, w_\text{D}, w_\text{E}, w_\text{C})$ are calibrated against historical district-level non-performing loan (NPL) rates from Bangladesh Bank, and the CRSI term enters with a negative sign because high crop rotation stability reduces credit risk.

Calibration of ACRS against Bangladesh Bank NPL data requires a historical training period of at least 5 years of concurrent satellite and NPL data. Phase A of the research agenda (Months 1–8) initiates this calibration using existing satellite archives (Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 available from 2015–2016) matched against Bangladesh Bank district NPL series.

### 7.2 NbS Impact on ACRS: Measuring the Financial Feedback Loop

The financial feedback loop (Part 3, Section 7) requires demonstrating that HydroECA gains cause measurable ACRS reductions. The measurement protocol is:

1. **Pre-intervention ACRS baseline:** Established for treatment and control Union Parishads at Phase D t₀ using the synthetic control weighting ($W^*$) from the Phase D DiD framework.
2. **Post-intervention ACRS tracking:** Monthly ACRS updates for treatment and control UPs throughout the 24-month Phase D window.
3. **ACRS-NPL linkage validation:** Concurrent collection of district-level NPL rates from Bangladesh Bank, testing whether ACRS changes predict NPL changes with the expected lag structure (3–12 months from satellite observation to loan performance realisation).
4. **Provisioning impact estimation:** Modelling of Bangladesh Bank regulatory provisioning implications of NPL changes, using published provisioning rate schedules.

This protocol produces the quantitative evidence base required for climate-finance instrument design: if NbS interventions in treatment UPs generate measurable ACRS reductions that translate into measurable NPL reductions, the mechanism for pricing parametric insurance and green bonds against NbS performance is established.

### 7.3 Insurance Sector Integration

The SII and FEI components of the ACRS are directly relevant to parametric crop insurance products, where payout triggers are defined against observable indices rather than loss assessment. SARE's SII and FEI are operationally suitable as parametric triggers: they are objective, satellite-derived, transparent, updatable at policy-relevant frequencies, and independently verifiable by any party with access to the underlying Sentinel data. The financial-sector output layer provides the computational infrastructure for parametric insurance product design aligned with the NbS programme's salinity and flood risk reduction objectives.

## 8. OPERATIONAL PROCESSING INFRASTRUCTURE

### 8.1 Processing Pipeline Architecture

SARE's operational pipeline is structured as five sequential processing stages:

```
STAGE 1: DATA ACQUISITION
  Sentinel-1/2 scene download via ESA Copernicus Data Space
  BMD, BWDB, GPM IMERG ingestion
  Scene scheduling and cloud cover flagging
           │
           ▼
STAGE 2: PREPROCESSING
  Radiometric calibration (Sentinel-1 SNAP toolbox)
  Atmospheric correction (Sentinel-2: Sen2Cor / ACOLITE for water surfaces)
  Geometric co-registration to 10 m grid
  Cloud and shadow masking (Sentinel-2 SCL layer + MAJA)
           │
           ▼
STAGE 3: INDEX COMPUTATION
  Per-index algorithms (Section 3) applied to preprocessed imagery
  Uncertainty map computation (Section 4)
  Quality control flags applied
           │
           ▼
STAGE 4: AGGREGATION AND FORMATTING
  Resampling to 100 m (micro-agent) and 1 km (macro-agent) tensor grids
  EnKF observation vector assembly with uncertainty covariance
  Financial-sector ACRS computation and district aggregation
  Phase classifier computation for seasonal graph switching
           │
           ▼
STAGE 5: DELIVERY
  State tensor update push to EnKF assimilation engine (GPU VRAM ring buffer)
  ACRS delivery to financial-sector API endpoint
  Dashboard update for Union Parishad and district governance interfaces
  Archive to national NbS data repository
```

### 8.2 Latency and Throughput

The operational latency target — index delivery within 24 hours of satellite overpass — requires the following processing throughput:

- Sentinel-1 IW scene (Bangladesh coverage, dual polarisation): ~4 GB per scene; 2 scenes per 6-day cycle.
- Sentinel-2 L1C scene (Bangladesh coverage): ~800 MB per scene; up to 4 scenes per 5-day cycle.
- Full pipeline processing time (all 7 indices, national domain): target $\leq 4$ hours on the operational compute infrastructure.
- Peak demand period: monsoon onset (May–June), when rapid flood extent change requires maximum FEI update frequency.

### 8.3 Infrastructure Requirements

| Component | Specification |
| :--- | :--- |
| Primary compute | 32-core CPU server, 256 GB RAM, 4× enterprise GPU for deep learning classifier inference |
| Storage | 500 TB NAS for rolling 5-year satellite archive; 50 TB NVMe for active processing |
| Network | Dedicated fibre uplink for Sentinel data download (peak 2 Gbps during scene acquisition windows) |
| Software stack | ESA SNAP (SAR), Sen2Cor (S2 atmospheric correction), GDAL/OGR, Python (NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn, PyTorch for ML classifiers), PostgreSQL/PostGIS for geospatial data management |
| Hosting | National data centre (preferred: BWDB or ICT Division facility) with UPS and redundant connectivity |

SARE's compute infrastructure is separable from the HRL platform's GPU cluster (specified in the HRL technical baseline). The two systems communicate via a well-defined API at Stage 5 of the SARE pipeline. This separation enables independent scaling, maintenance, and procurement of each infrastructure component.

## 9. PHASED IMPLEMENTATION SCHEDULE

SARE's implementation is phased to align with the NbS programme's research agenda while delivering early-stage value from existing satellite archives.

### Phase A (Months 1–8): Archive Processing and Pilot Calibration

- Process Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 archives (2016–2026) for 5 pilot districts.
- Calibrate FEI, SII, EHI, SMVI against BWDB historical records.
- Calibrate CRSI classifier using BBS agricultural census and field survey ground truth.
- Initiate ACRS calibration against Bangladesh Bank district NPL series (2016–2026).
- Deliver: Pilot district index time series (2016–2026); initial uncertainty parameterisation; ACRS training dataset.

### Phase B (Months 6–18): Operational Pipeline and National Rollout

- Deploy operational processing pipeline (Stage 1–5 infrastructure).
- Extend index computation to national domain.
- Commission EnKF observation vector delivery API.
- Commence quarterly validation reporting.
- Deliver: Full national SARE index stream operational; EnKF integration live; financial-sector API operational.

### Phase C (Months 14–28): RL Environment Integration and Training Support

- Provide real-time SARE index stream to RL training environment.
- Supply phase classifier outputs for seasonal graph switching.
- Deliver resistance layer update feeds to HydroECA computation module.
- Commence CCCI-based NbS outcome tracking at pilot sites.
- Deliver: Calibrated resistance layer for pilot districts; real-time seasonal phase flags; CCCI outcome tracking for Phase C pilot sites.

### Phase D (Months 24–48): Attribution and Financial Validation

- Provide SARE index time series for Phase D DiD treatment and control UPs.
- Track ACRS changes in treatment vs. control UPs throughout pilot.
- Validate ACRS-NPL linkage against concurrent Bangladesh Bank data.
- Deliver: Phase D SARE attribution dataset; ACRS-NPL validation report; parametric insurance trigger design specifications.

## 10. LIMITATIONS AND OPEN QUESTIONS

### 10.1 SAR Penetration Under Dense Vegetation

C-band SAR signal penetration under dense vegetation canopy is limited. In mature mangrove stands and established riparian buffers — precisely the NbS intervention types being monitored — canopy closure reduces SAR sensitivity to ground surface conditions. This means that SARE's ability to detect inundation, soil moisture change, and erosion hazard beneath established NbS vegetation will degrade as interventions mature. L-band SAR (e.g., ALOS-2 PALSAR-2, or the forthcoming NISAR mission) has significantly better canopy penetration and should be integrated into SARE's sensor architecture when L-band operational data becomes consistently available at national coverage.

### 10.2 Salinity Index Limitations in Highly Turbid Waters

The GBM delta carries extremely high suspended sediment loads, particularly during monsoon. High turbidity substantially alters water spectral properties and degrades the accuracy of spectral salinity retrieval. The NDSI calibration against in-situ EC measurements is conducted across the full range of turbidity conditions, and the regression model includes a turbidity co-variate. However, at extreme turbidity levels (suspended sediment concentration $> 500\,\text{mg/L}$, common in the Jamuna and Padma channels during peak monsoon), SII retrievals should be treated as indicative rather than quantitative.

### 10.3 EHI Temporal Resolution vs. Morphodynamic Event Timescales

The GBM delta's most severe erosion events — sudden bank collapses, avulsion, char formation following cyclone impacts — can occur within hours. SARE's 6-day Sentinel-1 repeat and monthly EHI update cycle cannot capture these events in near-real-time. The HITL escalation protocol (Stage 2 Red Alert) is the system's primary safeguard against this gap: the 72-hour field audit requirement triggered at Yellow Alert ($\sigma^\text{morph} \geq 0.65$) is designed to deploy ground-truth observation before the next satellite overpass in rapidly evolving situations.

### 10.4 ACRS Calibration Data Availability

The ACRS calibration against Bangladesh Bank district NPL rates requires consistent, district-level NPL data at monthly frequency over the 2016–2026 archive period. The availability and granularity of Bangladesh Bank's historical NPL series at district level is not confirmed. If district-level NPL data is unavailable or inconsistently reported prior to 2020, the ACRS calibration period will be shortened, reducing statistical confidence in the NPL linkage validation. This data availability question should be resolved in Phase A.

### 10.5 Open-Access Data Policy

SARE is specified as a national public infrastructure using open-access satellite data (Sentinel, MODIS, GPM). Its outputs should be made publicly available through a national data portal to maximise utility across the agricultural, insurance, banking, and academic sectors. The governance framework for this data portal — access controls, update schedules, API standards, and licensing — requires specification in Phase A in coordination with the ICT Division and relevant ministries. Restricting SARE index access to the NbS programme alone would substantially understate the system's national economic value.

## 11. CONCLUSION

SARE resolves the primary data challenge in the RL-NbS synthesis: the requirement for a continuously updated, spatially explicit, uncertainty-quantified representation of Bangladesh's hydrological and agricultural state at the resolution and frequency required by the 14-channel state tensor and the directed HydroECA computation module.

Its seven indices collectively characterise the delta's hydrological connectivity (FEI), soil permeability dynamics (SMVI), drought exposure (DSI), salinity intrusion (SII), morphodynamic volatility (EHI), agricultural stability (CRSI), and ecological restoration progress (CCCI). These indices feed six of the fourteen state tensor channels directly, provide the dynamic inputs to the directed resistance layer, drive the seasonal graph switching mechanism, supply the EnKF observation vector, and generate the district-level credit risk scores required for the financial feedback loop.

The cloud-gap strategy — SAR primacy during monsoon, optical-SAR fusion during dry season, MODIS gap-fill for daily anomaly detection — ensures that SARE continues to deliver operationally useful outputs throughout Bangladesh's monsoon season, when hydrological risk is greatest and the RL system's adaptive management function is most needed.

As a national data infrastructure serving the NbS programme, the financial sector, Union Parishad governance systems, and the Bangladesh Water Development Board simultaneously, SARE generates value well beyond its role in the digital-twin computational layer. Its open-access design and national hosting model ensure that this value accrues to Bangladesh's institutions broadly, rather than being locked within a single programme boundary.

Index derivation algorithms, calibration parameters, and infrastructure specifications are subject to revision following Phase A data availability assessment and stakeholder review. The SARE specification has not been independently reviewed by satellite remote sensing specialists; such review is recommended before Phase B operational pipeline procurement.*
