## ABSTRACT

Bangladesh faces acute hydrological pressures — monsoon variability, riverbank erosion, coastal salinity intrusion — that generate recurrent economic losses across agriculture, banking, and insurance. A national Nature-Based Solutions (NbS) framework has been proposed as a low-cost, community-driven response, but its implementation architecture remains heuristic: priority intervention types are identified, but optimal spatial placement is not determined. Separately, a reinforcement learning (RL) framework for Miyawaki corridor restoration has been developed for ecological defragmentation of logged landscapes, using graph-theoretic connectivity optimisation and a spatially explicit multi-channel landscape tensor.

This paper assesses the conceptual fit between these two frameworks and proposes a synthesis: an RL-optimised spatial planning layer for hydrological NbS in Bangladesh. I argue that the core mathematical architecture of the [*Miyawaki RL system*](https://github.com/WilliamMurray-research/2026-0003-D/blob/main/ppr/019.md) — Equivalent Connected Area maximisation, resistance-weighted movement probability, and multi-channel state tensor — translates directly to hydrological connectivity optimisation, with modifications to state variables, reward structure, and transition dynamics. The standard ecological ECA metric requires reformulation as a directed, asymmetric graph measure — HydroECA — to reflect the anisotropic physics of water flow and the regime-switching behaviour of delta systems under flood and dry-season conditions. I further argue that the Bangladesh NbS paper's governance and monitoring architecture addresses the most significant weakness of the RL framework — the treatment of socio-political constraints as static masks rather than dynamic variables — through the introduction of a continuous Governance Feasibility Index (GFI); and that the GFI itself introduces a feedback endogeneity problem that requires an explicit exploration mechanism. The synthesis offers a spatially rigorous, governance-aware, and community-grounded approach to Bangladesh's hydrological crisis. We identify five development priorities, note three additional technical requirements arising from v0.1 review, and propose a phased research agenda.

**Keywords:** nature-based solutions, reinforcement learning, hydrological connectivity, Bangladesh, Miyawaki, spatial optimisation, governance, HydroECA, directed graph, morphodynamics

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Bangladesh sits at the confluence of three great river systems — the Ganges, Brahmaputra, and Meghna — draining a catchment area of nearly two million square kilometres into a delta that covers most of the country's southern lowlands. This position makes Bangladesh among the most hydrologically dynamic and hazard-exposed nations on earth. Monsoon variability, accelerated Himalayan glacial melt, riverbank erosion, and coastal salinity intrusion generate annual economic losses estimated at between 0.5 and 1 per cent of GDP, with cascading effects across agriculture, insurance, and banking.

My proposed national NbS framework addresses these pressures through ecological intervention: riparian buffer zones, floodplain restoration, wetland expansion, mangrove rehabilitation, and agro-ecological water-retention systems. The framework is economically well-grounded, governance-aware, and explicitly phased. Its central weakness, however, is spatial: it identifies what to plant and broadly where, but provides no optimisation mechanism for determining the precise configuration of interventions that would maximise hydrological benefit across a heterogeneous landscape.

Separately, an RL framework developed for ecological defragmentation of logged landscapes — the Miyawaki corridor optimisation system — offers precisely this kind of spatial optimisation engine. It models landscape connectivity as a graph-theoretic problem, trains an agent to maximise Equivalent Connected Area through sequential planting decisions, and produces spatially explicit corridor placement maps, species mix recommendations, and canopy closure projections.

This paper asks a simple question: can the Miyawaki RL framework be adapted to serve as the spatial planning layer for Bangladesh's NbS programme? I argue that the conceptual fit is strong, that the mathematical translation is tractable with necessary modifications, and that the synthesis would address the most significant gap in each framework — spatial indeterminacy in the Bangladesh NbS paper, and dynamic governance constraints in the Miyawaki RL system.

### 1.1 Paper Structure

Section 2 characterises the structural gaps in each source framework. Section 3 assesses conceptual and mathematical fit, incorporating the directed-graph reformulation of HydroECA, GFI endogeneity treatment, and morphodynamic transition model extension. Section 4 proposes the core synthesis architecture. Section 5 identifies seven development priorities (five from v0.1 plus two new). Section 6 outlines a phased research agenda. Section 7 acknowledges limitations and open questions. Section 8 concludes.

## 2. STRUCTURAL GAPS IN EACH FRAMEWORK

### 2.1 The Bangladesh NbS Framework: Spatial Indeterminacy

The national NbS framework is comprehensive in its economic logic, governance structure, and monitoring design. It correctly identifies the priority intervention types — riparian buffers, floodplain reconnection, wetland restoration, sediment-smart river training, mangrove expansion, and agro-ecological water retention — and grounds each in international cost-effectiveness evidence. Its implementation framework is appropriately phased across a 25-year horizon with defined policy-revision triggers.

What the framework does not provide is a spatial optimisation mechanism. The question of where, precisely, to place a riparian buffer to maximise flood attenuation across a river system; which wetland restoration sites would generate the greatest increase in dry-season water availability; or how to sequence interventions across a heterogeneous delta landscape to produce network-level hydrological benefits — these questions are left to expert judgment and district-level masterplans. This is a significant gap. Landscape-scale hydrological systems exhibit strong non-linearities and connectivity effects: the benefit of an individual intervention depends critically on its position within the broader network. Heuristic placement risks substantial underperformance relative to optimised placement.

The framework acknowledges this implicitly in its monitoring design, which tracks reductions in flood extent and erosion rates as performance indicators, and defines policy-revision triggers for hydrological underperformance. But it provides no mechanism for translating underperformance signals into spatially revised intervention strategies. The monitoring system can detect failure; it cannot diagnose its geometric cause or prescribe a spatial remedy.

### 2.2 The Miyawaki RL Framework: Static Governance Constraints

The Miyawaki corridor optimisation framework is technically sophisticated. Its multi-channel landscape tensor captures ecological, climatic, and feasibility variables simultaneously. Its reward function integrates connectivity gains, geometric efficiency, ecological maturity, and cost. Its transition model simulates canopy growth, soil recovery, microclimate propagation, species interactions, and disturbance regimes. The Monte Carlo Tree Search architecture enables long-horizon planning under uncertainty.

Its principal weakness is the treatment of socio-political constraints. These are encoded as a static feasibility mask — Channel 7 of the state tensor — that prohibits interventions in areas with adverse land tenure, cultural protections, or legal restrictions. This is a reasonable first approximation for logged forest landscapes, where legal tenure is often relatively well-defined and stable.

In Bangladesh, however, socio-political constraints are neither static nor well-defined. Land tenure in floodplain areas is contested and seasonally variable. Community governance structures — Union Parishads, cooperative societies, haor management committees — mediate access to wetlands, riverbanks, and coastal zones in ways that change with political cycles, seasonal flooding, and economic pressure. The feasibility mask as currently conceived cannot capture these dynamics. An optimisation agent trained on static constraints will systematically overestimate the implementability of its recommendations and underestimate the time and transaction costs of community engagement.

The framework acknowledges this limitation explicitly in Section 7.3, noting that the agent "cannot resolve socio-political conflicts or negotiate access rights." It defers resolution to "further research." That further research is, in effect, the governance architecture developed in the Bangladesh NbS paper.

### 2.3 Summary: Complementary Gaps

| Framework | Core Strength | Critical Gap |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bangladesh NbS Paper** | Economic logic, governance architecture, monitoring design, community grounding | Spatial optimisation: where to intervene for maximum hydrological benefit |
| **Miyawaki RL System** | Spatial optimisation engine, connectivity mathematics, transition modelling, reward design | Dynamic governance constraints: socio-political feasibility as a variable, not a mask |
| **Proposed Synthesis** | Spatially rigorous, governance-aware, hydrologically grounded | Development priority: validated for Bangladesh hydrology and community governance |

## 3. CONCEPTUAL AND MATHEMATICAL FIT ASSESSMENT

### 3.1 The Core Structural Analogy

The Miyawaki RL framework is built on a foundational insight: forest defragmentation is a sequential spatial decision problem in which each intervention modifies the landscape's connectivity graph, and the long-term ecological benefit depends on the cumulative geometric configuration of interventions rather than their individual properties. The Go analogy — each planting action alters the strategic landscape for all subsequent actions — captures this insight precisely.

Hydrological NbS in Bangladesh exhibits the same structural property. Each riparian buffer, restored wetland, or reconnected floodplain modifies the hydrological connectivity of the river network. A riparian buffer planted upstream of a flood-prone confluence has different hydraulic consequences than the same buffer planted downstream. A wetland restored in a low-lying area that serves as a natural overflow sink generates different network-level benefits than one restored in an upland fringe. The position, geometry, and sequencing of interventions determine system-level outcomes. This is a sequential spatial decision problem of exactly the kind for which the Miyawaki RL architecture was designed.

### 3.2 Mathematical Translation: HydroECA as a Directed Metric

#### 3.2.1 The Directionality Problem

The Miyawaki framework's core metric — Equivalent Connected Area (ECA) — measures the effective connected area of a habitat network, weighting patch areas by movement probability derived from symmetric resistance surfaces. This formalism applies an isotropic distance-decay function: movement probability between nodes $i$ and $j$ equals movement probability between $j$ and $i$.

Hydrological flow is not isotropic. Water follows hydraulic gradients and is subject to:

- **Directional gravity forcing:** flow probability from an upland node to a downstream wetland node is structurally higher than the reverse.
- **Backwater effects:** under flood conditions, downstream inundation can raise water levels and reverse the effective connectivity of reaches that are unidirectional under normal flow. In the GBM delta, this is not an edge case; it is a routine monsoon-season condition affecting large portions of the floodplain.
- **Seasonal regime switching:** the directionality of the connectivity network changes substantively between monsoon and dry seasons. A directed graph parameterised for the dry season will misrepresent flood-season connectivity and vice versa.

Applying the symmetric ECA formulation to a hydrological domain would produce systematically incorrect connectivity estimates — overstating the benefit of interventions in locations that appear well-connected under one directional regime but are isolated under another, and understating interventions that bridge connectivity across seasonal regime transitions.

#### 3.2.2 HydroECA: Formal Definition

We define **Hydrological Equivalent Connected Area (HydroECA)** using an asymmetric, directed graph formulation:

$$\text{HydroECA} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_i \, a_j \, p_{ij}^*}$$

where:

- $a_i$, $a_j$ are the storage or attenuation capacities of nodes $i$ and $j$ (measured in volumetric units normalised to area equivalents);
- $p_{ij}^*$ is the **directed transmission probability** of water flow from node $i$ to node $j$ along the least-resistance path through the network — the hydrological analogue of movement probability in the ecological ECA formulation.

The critical departure from standard ECA is that $p_{ij}^* \neq p_{ji}^*$ in general: transmission probability is asymmetric, reflecting hydraulic gradient, channel geometry, and resistance along the directed path.

#### 3.2.3 Directed Transmission Probability

The directed transmission probability $p_{ij}^*$ is computed as:

$$p_{ij}^* = \exp\left(-\theta \cdot R_{ij}^*\right)$$

where $R_{ij}^*$ is the **least-resistance directed path cost** from $i$ to $j$ across the resistance surface, and $\theta$ is a scale parameter calibrated to observed flood routing data. The directed path cost is computed on a directed graph in which edge weights encode hydraulic resistance — a function of slope, channel geometry, land cover, soil permeability, and embankment presence — with directional asymmetry imposed by slope and hydraulic gradient.

#### 3.2.4 Seasonal Regime Switching

Because directionality reverses between flow regimes, HydroECA must be computed separately for monsoon and dry-season states. We define:

$$\text{HydroECA}_{\text{monsoon}}$$  and  $$\text{HydroECA}_{\text{dry}}$$

as season-specific metrics computed on season-appropriate directed resistance graphs. The overall RL reward function combines both, weighted by the relative management priority assigned to flood attenuation versus dry-season water availability — a policy parameter set by Bangladesh NbS programme managers rather than the RL agent.

The transition between seasonal graphs is not binary; transitional periods (April–May and October–November) require interpolated resistance parameterisations. This is incorporated in the monsoon-aware temporal architecture (Section 3.5 and Priority 3).

### 3.3 State Tensor Adaptation and GFI Endogeneity

The Miyawaki framework's multi-channel state tensor requires systematic adaptation. The existing ten channels encode ecological and climatic variables appropriate for forest restoration. A hydrological NbS tensor for Bangladesh requires a substantially different channel structure, though the tensor architecture itself is directly reusable.

| Channel | Miyawaki Original | Bangladesh Hydrological Adaptation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0 | Canopy density (0–1) | Floodplain connectivity index (0–1) |
| 1 | Soil health index (0–1) | Soil permeability / infiltration rate (0–1) |
| 2 | Road scar mask (binary) | Embankment / hard infrastructure mask (binary) |
| 3 | Microclimate humidity (0–1) | Seasonal inundation depth (normalised) |
| 4 | Habitat classification (categorical) | Land use / NbS intervention type (categorical) |

#### 3.3.1 The Governance Feasibility Index

The most significant structural change is to Channel 7. In the Miyawaki framework, the feasibility mask is binary and static: a cell is either available for intervention or it is not. In the Bangladesh adaptation, governance feasibility is a continuous, dynamic variable — the **Governance Feasibility Index (GFI)** — that encodes the current estimated probability of successful community engagement, land access negotiation, and institutional cooperation in a given spatial cell. The GFI integrates: land tenure clarity and dispute status; Union Parishad engagement level; community cooperative participation; ministerial coordination status for the relevant district; seasonal access conditions; and historical NbS adoption rates in adjacent cells.

The GFI is updated between planning cycles by field teams, community liaison officers, and institutional coordinators. High GFI values signal cells where interventions can be implemented immediately; low GFI values signal cells where governance groundwork must precede physical intervention.

#### 3.3.2 GFI Endogeneity and the Exploration Problem

A structural problem not addressed in v0.1 arises from the dynamic nature of the GFI. The RL agent's recommendations will influence GFI values through the concentration of community engagement resources: if the agent consistently recommends high-GFI cells, engagement effort concentrates there, raising those cells' GFI further. Low-GFI cells in strategically important locations receive no engagement effort, their GFI stagnates, and the agent continues to avoid them. The result is a self-reinforcing spatial concentration of intervention that may be politically convenient but hydrologically suboptimal.

This is an exploration-exploitation problem in the governance domain. The standard RL response is an exploration bonus. We introduce a **GFI exploration bonus** $\beta_{\text{GFI}}$ added to the reward for interventions in cells with GFI values below a threshold $\tau_{\text{GFI}}$ but with high latent HydroECA value:

$$r_{\text{explore}}(s, a) = \beta_{\text{GFI}} \cdot \mathbb{1}\left[\text{GFI}(s) < \tau_{\text{GFI}}\right] \cdot \Delta\text{HydroECA}^*(s, a)$$

where $\Delta\text{HydroECA}^*(s, a)$ is the counterfactual HydroECA gain that would result from action $a$ at state $s$ if GFI were at its maximum value. This term encourages the agent to recommend early governance investment in high-value but currently inaccessible cells, generating a sequenced strategy in which governance groundwork precedes physical intervention in strategically important areas.

The parameters $\beta_{\text{GFI}}$ and $\tau_{\text{GFI}}$ are policy hyperparameters calibrated against programme capacity constraints. Setting $\beta_{\text{GFI}} = 0$ recovers the v0.1 behaviour; increasing it produces more aggressive exploration of low-GFI high-value cells.

### 3.4 Reward Function Adaptation

The Miyawaki reward function integrates connectivity gain, geometric efficiency, ecological maturity, and cost. The Bangladesh adaptation requires modifications to each component while preserving the overall structure.

- **Connectivity component:** Shifts from ecological ECA to **HydroECA** (directed formulation, Section 3.2): the reward for a planting or restoration action is proportional to the increase in effective connected floodplain area, weighted by directed hydrological transmission probability. Seasonal weights apply: monsoon-season HydroECA gain and dry-season HydroECA gain enter the reward with programme-specified weights reflecting flood attenuation versus water availability priorities.
- **Geometric efficiency component:** Reinterpreted as hydraulic efficiency: elongated, narrow riparian buffers that fail to connect larger floodplain units are penalised relative to configurations that bridge high-storage wetland nodes.
- **Maturity component:** Replaced by a **hydrological effectiveness index** that tracks soil permeability recovery, riparian canopy establishment, and sediment stabilisation.
- **Cost component:** Adapted to reflect Bangladesh-specific implementation costs, including community engagement time, land negotiation, and monsoon-season access constraints.
- **GFI exploration bonus:** As defined in Section 3.3.2.
- **Salinity intrusion penalty:** Rewards interventions that reduce coastal salinity penetration, reflecting the specific coastal vulnerability of southern Bangladesh.
- **Co-benefit bonus:** Rewards interventions that generate measurable agricultural productivity gains alongside hydrological benefits, reflecting the Bangladesh NbS framework's emphasis on multi-sectoral returns.

### 3.5 Transition Model Adaptation

The Miyawaki transition model simulates canopy growth, soil recovery, microclimate propagation, species interactions, and disturbance regimes. The Bangladesh adaptation requires a **hydrological transition model** that simulates floodplain reconnection dynamics, riparian vegetation establishment, wetland recovery, and sediment transport. The mathematical structure of the transition model — logistic growth with lateral spillover, diffusion-based propagation, stochastic disturbance — is directly reusable with domain-appropriate parameterisation.

#### 3.5.1 Monsoon-Aware Temporal Structure

The Bangladesh adaptation must distinguish between:

- **Monsoon season (June–October):** Flood dynamics dominate; physical intervention is constrained; HydroECA$_{\text{monsoon}}$ governs reward computation.
- **Dry season (November–May):** Planting and restoration are feasible; HydroECA$_{\text{dry}}$ governs reward computation.
- **Transitional periods (April–May, October–November):** Interpolated resistance parameterisations; partial action masking.

This seasonal structure introduces a temporal dimension to the feasibility mask that the Miyawaki framework does not require.

#### 3.5.2 Morphodynamic Disturbance Module

The GBM delta is among the most morphodynamically active systems on earth. Riverbank erosion and char land formation operate on timescales of months to a few years — well within the planning horizon. A static resistance layer will become misleading faster than the transition model can compensate through ecological succession alone.

A **stochastic morphodynamic disturbance module** is added to the transition model. At each timestep, the resistance surface and tensor Channels 0, 5, 10, and 13 are updated by sampling from a disturbance distribution parameterised from BWDB historical erosion and accretion time series. The disturbance distribution is spatially structured — erosion and accretion rates vary systematically by geomorphological zone (active channel, char, stable floodplain, coastal fringe) — and is updated annually as new BWDB monitoring data become available.

This is not a full morphodynamic model, which would be computationally prohibitive within the RL training loop. It is a reduced-form stochastic emulator that propagates observed variability in landscape change into the transition model's uncertainty budget. Channel 13 (Morphodynamic change rate) provides the RL agent with a signal of local geomorphological volatility, allowing it to discount heavily the long-term benefit of interventions in high-erosion-risk cells.

## 4. THE PROPOSED SYNTHESIS

### 4.1 Architecture Overview

The proposed synthesis integrates the Miyawaki RL framework as a spatial planning layer within the Bangladesh NbS implementation architecture. The RL agent operates as an optimisation engine that takes landscape state data as input and produces spatially explicit intervention recommendations as output. These recommendations are then mediated through the governance and community engagement structures defined in the Bangladesh NbS framework before field implementation.

The RL agent does not replace human judgment or community decision-making. It operates as a decision-support tool that identifies high-value intervention opportunities and sequencing strategies — including governance investment sequences in currently low-GFI cells — that human planners and community groups can evaluate, modify, and implement. This is consistent with the Bangladesh NbS framework's emphasis on community ownership and the Miyawaki RL framework's acknowledgement that socio-political constraints cannot be resolved algorithmically.

### 4.2 Integration with the Bangladesh NbS Phase Structure

The synthesis maps naturally onto the three-phase implementation structure of the Bangladesh NbS framework.

- **Phase 1 (Years 1–3): Foundation.** The RL environment is constructed in parallel with NbS pilot deployment. Hydrological mapping activities generate the remote sensing and field data required to populate the state tensor, including initial directed resistance layer construction (Priority 1). Pilot project sites provide empirical data for transition model calibration, including morphodynamic disturbance parameterisation. Community engagement processes generate initial GFI values for pilot districts. The RL agent is trained on pilot-district data and produces optimised intervention sequences — including governance investment priorities in low-GFI high-value cells — for Phase 2 scaling decisions.

- **Phase 2 (Years 4–10): Environment Build.** RL-optimised corridor maps guide the spatial expansion of riparian buffers and wetland restoration. District-level NbS masterplans are generated through a combination of RL output and community participatory processes. The dynamic GFI is updated quarterly. Both HydroECA$_{\text{monsoon}}$ and HydroECA$_{\text{dry}}$ are tracked as primary performance indicators. Counterfactual attribution modelling is initiated for pilot districts (Section 7.4).

- **Phase 3 (Years 11–25): Adaptive Management.** The RL system operates as a continuous adaptive management tool, updating intervention recommendations in response to monitoring data, climate change signals, morphodynamic disturbance realisations, and governance dynamics. The long-term hydrological monitoring systems established in Phase 3 of the Bangladesh NbS framework provide the data streams required to keep the RL environment current.

### 4.3 The Governance Feasibility Index in Practice

The GFI for a given spatial cell encodes the current estimated probability of successful intervention implementation, integrating: land tenure clarity and dispute status; Union Parishad engagement level; community cooperative participation; ministerial coordination status for the relevant district; seasonal access conditions; and historical NbS adoption rates in adjacent cells.

The RL agent is trained to maximise long-term HydroECA subject to GFI constraints and with GFI exploration incentives. It learns to prefer high-GFI cells in the near term while investing, through the exploration bonus, in governance groundwork sequences in strategically important but currently low-GFI cells. This produces a planning strategy that is simultaneously ecologically optimal and politically realistic.

The GFI endogeneity mechanism (Section 3.3.2) ensures that the agent does not converge on the politically convenient path of exclusively targeting already-accessible cells. Instead, it generates intervention pipelines that pair near-term physical implementation in accessible areas with medium-term governance investment in high-value inaccessible areas — producing recommendations that are actionable today and strategically positioned for the next phase.

## 5. DEVELOPMENT PRIORITIES

### Priority 1: Directed Hydrological Resistance Layer Calibration

The translation of the Miyawaki resistance framework to directed hydrological resistance is the technical foundation of the entire synthesis. The directed resistance surface must encode hydraulic gradient, channel geometry, land cover, soil permeability, slope, and embankment presence, with asymmetric edge weights reflecting directional flow physics. Calibration requires observed flood routing and attenuation data from BWDB monitoring stations, satellite inundation time series, and targeted field campaigns.

Seasonal resistance parameterisation — separate directed graphs for monsoon and dry-season conditions, with interpolated transitional states — must be specified as part of this calibration exercise. This is Priority 1 because all downstream HydroECA calculations depend on the validity of the resistance layers.

### Priority 2: Governance Feasibility Index Operationalisation

The GFI concept requires careful operationalisation. Key challenges: identifying variables that best predict implementation success in Bangladesh's specific governance context; developing reliable, low-cost measurement protocols; designing update procedures that keep GFI values current without creating unsustainable data collection burdens; validating GFI predictions against observed implementation outcomes in pilot districts; and calibrating the exploration bonus parameters $\beta_{\text{GFI}}$ and $\tau_{\text{GFI}}$ against programme capacity constraints.

This work requires deep engagement with Union Parishad structures, community cooperative networks, and the Ministry of Water Resources. It is fundamentally a social science and governance research challenge. Priority 2 is to commission a GFI design study in collaboration with Bangladesh's leading political economy and governance researchers, with explicit treatment of the endogeneity problem and exploration mechanism.

### Priority 3: Monsoon-Aware Temporal Architecture

The RL environment must be redesigned around Bangladesh's monsoon temporal structure from the outset. This requires, at minimum:

- Explicit monsoon, dry-season, and transitional timestep definitions.
- Monsoon-phase action masking that prevents the agent from recommending physical interventions during active flooding.
- Seasonal HydroECA switching between directed monsoon and dry-season graph parameterisations.
- Transitional interpolation protocols for resistance surfaces.
- Dry-season planning windows during which community engagement, planting, and restoration activities concentrate.

### Priority 4: Multi-Species Riparian Composition Optimisation

Species selection in the Bangladesh hydrological context interacts with hydrological function in ways the Miyawaki framework does not model: root architecture determines bank stabilisation capacity; canopy density determines evapotranspiration and shade cooling; stem density determines flow resistance and sediment capture. Species selection is therefore a hydraulic engineering consideration as well as an ecological one.

Priority 4 is to develop a **hydrologically informed species mix recommendation module** integrating Miyawaki multi-strata planting principles with hydraulic function data for Bangladesh's native riparian species. This module enriches the planting action from a binary decision to a multi-dimensional choice over species composition, density, and strata configuration.

### Priority 5: Community Feedback Integration with Bayesian Assimilation

The Miyawaki framework's validation protocol relies on technical monitoring data (LiDAR, soil sensors, camera traps). Bangladesh's NbS programme is explicitly community-governed: Union Parishads and cooperatives are the primary implementation and monitoring agents.

The community feedback architecture must translate community-generated observations — crop yield records, flood depth measurements, soil condition assessments, fish catch data — into model inputs compatible with the RL environment's tensor structure. Critically, it must incorporate a **Bayesian data assimilation framework** that weights community observations by their uncertainty relative to instrument-grade monitoring data. Without this, the transition model will treat a Union Parishad estimate of flood depth with the same confidence as a BWDB gauge reading, generating miscalibrated transition dynamics and degraded agent performance.

The assimilation framework must be designed to be accessible to community members with varying levels of technical literacy while producing data of sufficient quality for model updating, and must return actionable, interpretable guidance to community users.

### Priority 6: Morphodynamic Disturbance Module Parameterisation

The stochastic morphodynamic disturbance module (Section 3.5.2) requires systematic parameterisation from BWDB historical erosion and accretion data, stratified by geomorphological zone. Key deliverables: zone classification map for high-risk districts; disturbance rate distributions by zone; update protocol for annual integration of new BWDB data; and calibration of Channel 13 (Morphodynamic change rate) against observed landscape change.

This priority does not require a full morphodynamic model. The objective is a reduced-form stochastic emulator that propagates realistic landscape change uncertainty into the transition model.

### Priority 7: Hierarchical RL Architecture for Computational Scaling

Delta-wide RL training across a spatially explicit multi-channel tensor, directed seasonal resistance graphs, dynamic GFI, monsoon temporal structure, and MCTS creates severe state-space explosion. A flat RL architecture at field-intervention resolution is computationally infeasible across the full spatial domain.

A **two-level hierarchical RL (HRL) architecture** is proposed:

1. **Macro-Agent:** Allocates NbS investment budgets across river sub-basins at coarse spatial resolution, optimising basin-level HydroECA and governance investment sequencing.
2. **Micro-Agent:** Optimises spatial geometry, species mix, and intervention timing within sub-basins at fine resolution, given boundary conditions passed from the macro-agent.

The resolution parameters for each level (candidate values: ~1 km² macro, ~100 m² micro) require stress-testing against boundary condition transmission fidelity before adoption. A three-order-of-magnitude resolution jump risks boundary artefacts at the macro-micro interface; intermediate resolution tiers should be evaluated if artefacts are significant. Cloud computing partnership requirements and model compression strategies should be scoped early in Phase B.

## 7. LIMITATIONS AND OPEN QUESTIONS

### 7.1 Data Availability

The RL environment requires high-resolution, spatially explicit hydrological data that may not be consistently available across all of Bangladesh's high-risk districts. The Bangladesh Water Development Board maintains extensive monitoring networks, but data quality, coverage, and accessibility vary. The directed resistance layer calibration (Priority 1) must include a realistic assessment of data gaps and a protocol for operating under data-sparse conditions, potentially using synthetic data augmentation during early training phases.

### 7.2 Computational Requirements

The HRL architecture (Priority 7) addresses the most acute scaling constraints, but boundary condition transmission between macro and micro levels introduces its own design challenges. Cloud computing partnerships should be assessed early in Phase B. The morphodynamic disturbance module is designed as a reduced-form emulator specifically to avoid the computational cost of full morphodynamic simulation within the training loop; this tradeoff requires explicit validation.

### 7.3 GFI Validity Risks and Endogeneity

The GFI is the synthesis's most innovative element and its most significant validity risk. Systematic bias — overestimating feasibility in politically sensitive areas, or failing to capture rapid governance deterioration following election cycles — will skew agent recommendations toward unimplementable interventions. The endogeneity feedback identified in Section 3.3.2 adds a second validity risk: even an unbiased GFI will generate concentrated interventions if the exploration bonus is miscalibrated. GFI design must include explicit mechanisms for detecting both systematic bias and endogeneity-driven concentration, including comparison of predicted and observed implementation rates across pilot districts and spatial audits of intervention geographic distribution.

### 7.4 Attribution of Hydrological Benefit

Attributing observed changes in flood extent, erosion rates, or dry-season water availability to specific NbS interventions is methodologically challenging in a complex delta system with multiple interacting drivers. The directed HydroECA framework offers an improvement over aggregate performance indicators by modelling the connectivity contribution of each intervention — but attribution from connectivity change to observed hydrological change requires a counterfactual modelling framework.

The research agenda must include, as a prerequisite for the adaptive management use case, either: (a) a counterfactual modelling framework in which unimplemented high-scoring RL recommendations serve as the control condition; or (b) a quasi-experimental pilot design in which matched districts receive and do not receive RL-guided interventions. Without one of these, the adaptive management loop cannot reliably update the transition model from monitoring data, and the claim that HydroECA enables attribution of network-level change to specific spatial decisions cannot be validated.

### 7.5 Community Acceptance of Algorithmic Recommendations

Community groups and Union Parishad representatives may be sceptical of intervention recommendations generated by an algorithmic system they did not participate in designing. The community feedback integration architecture (Priority 5) is partly a response to this risk, but co-design must be embedded across all phases. The research agenda includes co-design workshops with community representatives at each phase, ensuring that the RL system is understood, critiqued, and iteratively refined by the communities it is intended to serve.

The GFI exploration mechanism introduces an additional dimension of this challenge: the system will sometimes recommend governance investment in areas that community members and field teams consider unpromising. The rationale for such recommendations — high latent HydroECA value — must be communicable to non-technical audiences if it is to be accepted rather than overridden.

### 7.6 HydroECA Metric Sensitivity

The directed formulation of HydroECA (Section 3.2) depends critically on the scale parameter $\theta$ in the transmission probability decay function and on the resistance layer values. Sensitivity analysis of HydroECA to these parameters must be conducted during Phase B before the metric is used as a primary performance indicator or reward signal. Particular attention should be paid to the stability of HydroECA rankings across seasonal resistance graph variants — if the relative ordering of intervention sites reverses between monsoon and dry-season parameterisations, the seasonal weighting policy parameters will require careful calibration.

## 8. CONCLUSION

The conceptual fit between the Miyawaki RL corridor optimisation framework and the Bangladesh national NbS framework is strong. Both frameworks address the same underlying problem — how to optimise the spatial configuration of ecological interventions to maximise landscape-scale hydrological or ecological function — and their respective weaknesses are precisely complementary.

Version 0.2 strengthens the mathematical foundation of the synthesis in three respects. First, it formalises HydroECA as a directed, asymmetric metric computed on season-specific resistance graphs, correcting the isotropic assumption of the v0.1 formulation and accommodating the backwater and regime-switching behaviour characteristic of the GBM delta. Second, it identifies and addresses the GFI endogeneity problem through an explicit exploration bonus mechanism that prevents the self-reinforcing concentration of intervention in already-accessible cells. Third, it adds a morphodynamic disturbance module to the transition model, replacing the implicitly static resistance layer of v0.1 with a stochastic emulator calibrated to observed landscape change rates.

The most significant innovations required remain the **Governance Feasibility Index with exploration mechanism**, the **directed monsoon-aware HydroECA metric**, the **community feedback integration protocol with Bayesian assimilation**, and the **hierarchical RL architecture** for computational scaling. None of these require fundamental departures from the Miyawaki framework's computational architecture; all are tractable within the phased development agenda.

Two prerequisites for the adaptive management use case — a counterfactual attribution framework and HydroECA sensitivity analysis — are now explicit requirements in the Phase D validation agenda, ensuring that the transition model can be reliably updated from monitoring data and that the performance metric is robust before it enters the reward function.

Bangladesh's hydrological crisis is acute, and the window for cost-effective nature-based intervention is narrowing as climate pressures intensify and land use patterns become more entrenched. An RL-optimised spatial planning capability with governance-aware sequencing — including proactive investment in currently inaccessible high-value cells — could meaningfully increase both the hydrological return on NbS investment and the political durability of the programme by generating a pipeline of interventions that becomes progressively more implementable over time. This assessment continues to support the development effort.
