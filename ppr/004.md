## Abstract

Artificial intelligence systems capable of generating software artefacts have become embedded within contemporary engineering practice. Their adoption has accelerated the delegation of core cognitive tasks to opaque, stochastic models, thereby reshaping professional norms and expanding the risk landscape. This paper argues that artificial intelligence intensifies, rather than diminishes, the duty of care owed by engineers and organisations. Negligence doctrine already accommodates software failures, and artificial intelligence does not alter the analytical structure of duty, breach, causation, or damage. Instead, artificial intelligence amplifies foreseeability, increases the burden of reasonable precautions, and heightens the significance of organisational governance.

The analysis proceeds in twelve sections. It outlines the technological context in which artificial intelligence‑generated software is produced, sets out the universal structure of negligence law, and examines duty, breach, causation, and damage in artificial intelligence‑augmented engineering. It integrates industry standards, regulatory guidance, and documented failure modes to explain how courts determine the content of the reasonable engineer standard. It critically evaluates the divergence between negligence’s unitary reasonable precautions standard and the European Union’s risk‑tiered Artificial Intelligence Act. It examines equitable doctrines, including fiduciary duties, estoppel, unconscionability, and breach of confidence, and explains how these doctrines interact with negligence in practice. The paper concludes by addressing counter‑arguments that existing doctrine is insufficient and that artificial intelligence requires new liability categories.

## 2 Background and Context

Artificial intelligence systems capable of generating software artefacts have become embedded within engineering workflows. These systems produce code, configuration files, and decision logic with minimal human intervention. Their behaviour is non‑deterministic, their internal reasoning is opaque, and their outputs vary across identical prompts. These characteristics complicate verification and increase the difficulty of attributing responsibility when defects arise.

The adoption of artificial intelligence has coincided with declining code literacy among practitioners. Engineers increasingly rely on artificial intelligence systems to produce code without understanding the underlying logic. This reliance has eroded traditional verification practices, including manual review, structured testing, and static analysis. The resulting competence inversion, in which the tool appears more capable than the operator, has significant implications for negligence law. Under *Rogers v Whitaker* [44], professionals must exercise independent judgment and cannot defer to external assurances when risks are known. Engineers who rely uncritically on artificial intelligence systems fail to meet this standard.

Organisational incentives exacerbate these risks. Many organisations prioritise velocity, automation, and productivity over diligence, documentation, and oversight. Artificial intelligence systems are often deployed without adequate governance frameworks, training, or risk classification. These systemic deficiencies increase the likelihood of defects and complicate the allocation of responsibility when harm occurs.

Software increasingly governs physical systems, medical devices, industrial automation, and financial markets. Failures in these domains can cause physical injury, economic loss, and regulatory breaches. Courts treat such failures as engineering failures rather than philosophical debates about artificial intelligence. Negligence doctrine evaluates conduct according to the standard of the reasonable professional, and artificial intelligence does not alter this analytical structure. Instead, artificial intelligence amplifies foreseeability, increases the burden of reasonable precautions, and heightens the significance of organisational governance.

## 3 Negligence Law: The Universal Structure

Negligence doctrine across common‑law and civil‑law jurisdictions shares a common analytical structure. Although terminology differs, the core elements of duty, breach, causation, and damage remain consistent. Artificial intelligence‑generated software does not require a new liability regime because existing principles already accommodate software failures.

### 3.1 The Four Elements of Negligence

Common‑law jurisdictions require proof of four elements:
*   Duty of care
*   Breach of duty
*   Causation
*   Damage

These elements apply directly to artificial intelligence‑generated software. Engineers owe duties to users, clients, and the public. Failure to verify artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts constitutes breach. Artificial intelligence‑generated defects satisfy factual causation under *March v Stramare* [45]. Damage is assessed through established doctrines, including physical injury, economic loss, and regulatory harm.

### 3.2 Civil‑Law Alignment: Fault, Foreseeability, and Preventability

Civil‑law systems articulate negligence through the language of obligation, fault, causal link, and harm. German law under BGB §823 imposes liability for negligent infringement of protected interests. French law under Code civil art 1240 imposes liability for *faute*, defined as a breach of an objective duty of care. Japanese and Korean negligence doctrines similarly emphasise foreseeability and preventability.

The European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act strengthens this alignment by imposing risk‑tiered obligations, including documentation, logging, human oversight, and conformity assessments. These obligations codify what constitutes reasonable precautions. Failure to comply with these requirements is powerful evidence of fault under civil‑law negligence doctrines.

### 3.3 Software Engineering Within Negligence Doctrine

Courts treat software as either a product or a professional service depending on context. Both categories attract duties of care. Artificial intelligence does not create doctrinal novelty. Instead, artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts are absorbed into existing negligence frameworks. The opacity, unpredictability, and known failure modes of artificial intelligence systems increase foreseeability and expand the burden of reasonable precautions.

## 4 Duty of Care in Artificial Intelligence‑Augmented Engineering

The duty of care in negligence is determined objectively. Artificial intelligence does not diminish this duty. Instead, it intensifies it by introducing opacity, unpredictability, and known failure modes. Engineers and organisations must implement verification, documentation, and oversight mechanisms that reflect these heightened risks.

### 4.1 The Standard of Care

The standard of care is determined by what a reasonable professional would do in the circumstances. Under *Rogers v Whitaker* [46], professional standards are not set by average practice but by reasonable practice informed by contemporary knowledge. Artificial intelligence systems exhibit well‑documented limitations, including hallucination, insecure defaults, and non‑deterministic behaviour. A reasonable engineer must understand these limitations and implement precautions accordingly.

The High Court’s reasoning in *Imbree v McNeilly* [47] confirms that the standard does not adjust downward merely because a practitioner is inexperienced or reliant on tools. Delegating cognitive labour to artificial intelligence does not lower the standard; it raises it. Industry standards such as ISO/IEC 61508, IEC 62304, and MISRA C further inform the content of the duty by defining baseline expectations for documentation, verification, and testing.

### 4.2 Delegation to Artificial Intelligence Systems

Delegation does not discharge duty. In *Fennell v Robson Excavations* [48], the High Court held that a party who owes a duty of care cannot escape liability by delegating performance to another. This principle applies equally when the delegate is an artificial intelligence system. The doctrine of non‑delegable duties, articulated in *Kondis v State Transport Authority* [49], reinforces this conclusion. Where a relationship involves inherent risk, the duty is personal and cannot be shifted.

Artificial intelligence systems are tools with known limitations. Engineers must understand these limitations, verify outputs, and anticipate foreseeable risks. Failure to do so constitutes breach.

### 4.3 Foreseeability and Risk

Under *Wyong Shire Council v Shirt* [50], a risk is foreseeable if it is not far‑fetched or fanciful. Artificial intelligence failure modes are widely documented and therefore plainly foreseeable. The magnitude of harm associated with artificial intelligence‑generated defects, particularly in safety‑critical systems, increases the burden of reasonable precautions.

Professionals must anticipate risks inherent in their tools. Under *Hill v Van Erp* [51], failure to do so constitutes breach. Artificial intelligence systems require heightened oversight because their behaviour is opaque and non‑deterministic.

### 4.4 Organisational Duty

Organisations owe systemic duties independent of individual engineers. Under *Burnie Port Authority v General Jones* [52], entities controlling hazardous operations must implement systems that prevent harm. Artificial intelligence‑augmented engineering environments fall within this category. Organisations must implement governance frameworks, training, documentation, and oversight mechanisms. Failure to do so constitutes organisational negligence.

### 4.5 Summary

Artificial intelligence intensifies the duty of care by increasing foreseeability, expanding the burden of reasonable precautions, and heightening the significance of organisational governance. Negligence doctrine already accommodates artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts. The duty of care requires engineers and organisations to implement verification, documentation, and oversight mechanisms that reflect the heightened risks associated with artificial intelligence.

### 4.6 Determining the Content of the Reasonable Engineer Standard

Courts determine the content of the reasonable engineer standard through a combination of expert evidence, industry standards, regulatory guidance, documented failure modes, vendor disclaimers, and organisational policies. These evidentiary sources allow courts to ascertain what a reasonable engineer knew, or ought to have known, at the relevant time.

Industry standards such as ISO/IEC 61508, IEC 62304, and MISRA C do not constitute *per se* breach unless mandated by statute. However, non‑compliance is powerful evidence of breach because such standards codify what the profession regards as reasonable precautions. Under *Rogers v Whitaker*, professional practice informs but does not determine the standard of care. Standards therefore operate as evidentiary mechanisms through which courts assess foreseeability and reasonable precautions.

Regulatory guidance, including the European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act, the Food and Drug Administration’s artificial intelligence guidance, and the NIST Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework, further inform the standard by articulating governance expectations for high‑risk systems. Vendor disclaimers, which typically warn of hallucination, insecure outputs, and non‑deterministic behaviour, place engineers on notice of foreseeable risks. Documented failure modes, including hallucination, insecure defaults, and opaque provenance, reinforce the foreseeability of harm.

Together, these sources provide courts with a structured evidentiary basis for determining what a reasonable engineer knew and what precautions were required. Artificial intelligence therefore intensifies the standard of care not through doctrinal novelty, but through the expansion of professional knowledge and the increased availability of evidence demonstrating foreseeable risks.

## 5 Breach: How Artificial Intelligence Creates New Failure Modes

Breach is established when a defendant fails to take precautions that a reasonable professional would have taken in the circumstances. Under *Wyong Shire Council v Shirt* [53], breach requires consideration of foreseeability, probability, seriousness of harm, and the burden of precautions. Artificial intelligence‑generated software introduces failure modes that are not merely foreseeable but well‑documented, thereby expanding the range of precautions required of engineers and organisations.

### 5.1 Failure to Inspect Artificial Intelligence‑Generated Code

The most direct breach arises from failure to inspect or review artificial intelligence‑generated code. Reviewing code, whether written by a human or generated by an artificial intelligence system, is a low‑burden, high‑value precaution. Courts have consistently held that failure to perform basic verification constitutes breach, as illustrated in *Bankstown Foundry Pty Ltd v Braistina* [54], where failure to inspect equipment was treated as a clear departure from reasonable practice.

Under *Rogers v Whitaker* [55], the standard of care is determined by what a reasonable professional would do, not by common practice. Even if industry norms drift towards uncritical reliance on artificial intelligence outputs, the professional standard remains anchored in reasonableness. A reasonable engineer knows that artificial intelligence systems hallucinate, fabricate dependencies, and generate insecure patterns. Blind trust in artificial intelligence outputs is therefore a breach of duty.

### 5.2 Failure to Understand and Manage Model Limitations

Artificial intelligence systems exhibit well‑known failure modes, including hallucination, insecure defaults, non‑determinism, and opaque provenance. These limitations are documented in vendor materials, academic literature, and industry guidance. Under *Shirt*, such risks are plainly foreseeable. The seriousness of harm associated with artificial intelligence‑generated defects, particularly in safety‑critical systems, increases the obligation to adopt precautions.

Under *Hill v Van Erp* [56], professionals must anticipate risks inherent in their tools. Engineers who deploy artificial intelligence systems without understanding their limitations fail to meet this requirement. Failure to understand model behaviour therefore constitutes breach.

### 5.3 Failure to Maintain Human Oversight

Human oversight is a core precaution in safety‑critical engineering. Removing or weakening human review constitutes breach because it violates both professional standards and legal expectations. In *Romeo v Conservation Commission* [57], breach analysis turned on whether reasonable precautions were available and proportionate. Human‑in‑the‑loop review is a low‑burden precaution that mitigates high‑magnitude risks. Its absence is therefore unreasonable.

Where artificial intelligence systems control physical systems, such as vehicles, medical devices, or industrial automation, the operation may constitute a dangerous activity under *Burnie Port Authority v General Jones* [58]. In such contexts, duties are non‑delegable and require heightened precautions.

### 5.4 Over‑Reliance on Vendor Representations

Engineers increasingly rely on vendor claims about model safety, reliability, or enterprise‑grade performance. This reliance is legally precarious. Vendor disclaimers typically state that outputs may be inaccurate, insecure, or harmful. Such disclaimers make risks foreseeable and place engineers on notice. Under *Shirt*, reliance on assurances that contradict known risks is unreasonable. Under *Rogers v Whitaker*, professionals must exercise independent judgment and cannot defer to external assurances when risks are known.

Vendor representations do not break the chain of responsibility because engineers retain control over deployment, verification, and oversight. Over‑reliance on vendor claims is therefore a breach.

### 5.5 Systemic Breach: Organisational Failures

Breach is not limited to individual engineers. Organisations may commit systemic breaches by failing to implement adequate governance frameworks. Under *Burnie Port Authority*, entities controlling hazardous operations must adopt systems that prevent harm. Artificial intelligence‑augmented engineering environments fall within this category.

Systemic breaches include:
*   AI governance failures
*   inadequate training on artificial intelligence limitations
*   failure to implement audit trails
*   lack of separation of duties
*   insufficient testing and validation protocols

These failures constitute breach because they create foreseeable risks that reasonable organisations would mitigate.

### 5.6 Summary

Artificial intelligence systems introduce failure modes that are foreseeable, well‑documented, and easily mitigated through reasonable precautions. Failure to inspect outputs, understand limitations, maintain oversight, or implement governance frameworks constitutes breach under established negligence doctrine.

## 6 Causation: Linking Artificial Intelligence‑Generated Errors to Harm

Causation in negligence requires both factual causation and normative attribution of responsibility. Artificial intelligence‑generated software does not alter this structure. Instead, it introduces multi‑actor causal chains, opaque decision pathways, and stochastic error modes that intensify the need for careful analysis.

### 6.1 Factual Causation

Under *March v Stramare* (E & MH) Pty Ltd [59], factual causation is established if the defendant’s conduct was a necessary condition of the harm. Artificial intelligence‑generated defects satisfy this requirement where they contribute materially to harm. The presence of multiple contributing factors does not defeat causation. Artificial intelligence‑augmented systems frequently involve vendor model behaviour, engineer oversight failures, organisational governance failures, and deployment environment interactions. These are concurrent causes, not alternative causes.

### 6.2 Scope of Liability

Even where factual causation is established, liability depends on whether the harm falls within the scope of the defendant’s responsibility. In *Strong v Woolworths Ltd* [60], the High Court emphasised that scope of liability is a normative inquiry grounded in foreseeability and policy. Artificial intelligence unpredictability does not narrow scope of liability; it expands it. Engineers know, or ought to know, that artificial intelligence systems hallucinate, generate insecure patterns, behave non‑deterministically, lack provenance, and fail silently. These characteristics make failure foreseeable.

### 6.3 Intervening Acts

A defendant may argue that artificial intelligence behaviour constitutes a *novus actus interveniens*. This argument is doctrinally weak. In *Medlin v State Government Insurance Commission* [61], the High Court held that an intervening act must be extraordinary, unforeseeable, and independent of the defendant’s conduct. Artificial intelligence behaviour is none of these. Artificial intelligence systems are tools. Tools do not break chains of causation. Their failures are attributable to those who deploy them.

### 6.4 Multi‑Actor Causation

Artificial intelligence‑augmented engineering environments involve multiple actors. Australian law handles multi‑actor causation through apportionment, not exculpation. Engineers contribute to causation when they fail to review artificial intelligence‑generated code, fail to test outputs, fail to understand model limitations, or fail to maintain oversight. Organisations contribute when they lack governance frameworks, fail to train staff, incentivise unsafe deployment practices, or fail to implement audit trails. Vendors contribute when models contain latent defects or documentation is inadequate. Deployment environments may also contribute.

Under apportionment statutes, courts allocate responsibility among actors. No actor escapes liability merely because others contributed.

### 6.5 Summary

Artificial intelligence‑generated software does not create new causation doctrine. It intensifies existing doctrine by introducing concurrent causes, foreseeable failure modes, opaque decision pathways, and multi‑actor responsibility. Artificial intelligence does not break causation; it strengthens it.

## 7 Damage: The Real‑World Consequences of Artificial Intelligence‑Generated Failures

Damage in negligence encompasses physical harm, economic loss, and regulatory consequences. Artificial intelligence‑generated software can cause harm across all three categories. Courts treat such harm as engineering harm, and artificial intelligence does not alter the analytical structure of damage assessment.

### 7.1 Physical Harm

Artificial intelligence‑generated control logic can cause physical injury or death when deployed in vehicles, medical devices, industrial automation, or other safety‑critical systems. Physical harm triggers civil negligence, criminal negligence, and regulatory penalties. The magnitude of harm associated with artificial intelligence‑generated defects increases the burden of reasonable precautions.

### 7.2 Economic Loss

Artificial intelligence‑generated software can cause pure economic loss, particularly in financial systems, trading algorithms, and autonomous agents. Under Australian law, pure economic loss is actionable where reliance is reasonable and harm is foreseeable. The *Knight Capital* incident demonstrates that software defects can cause catastrophic economic loss. Artificial intelligence‑generated defects fall within this category.

### 7.3 Regulatory and Reputational Harm

Artificial intelligence failures often result in privacy breaches, compliance violations, and regulatory investigations. Regulatory harm includes penalties under sectoral regimes, including medical device regulation, financial regulation, and consumer protection law. Reputational harm arises when artificial intelligence‑generated defects undermine public trust or damage organisational credibility.

### 7.4 Summary

Artificial intelligence‑generated software can cause physical harm, economic loss, and regulatory consequences. Negligence doctrine already accommodates these categories of harm. Artificial intelligence does not require a new damage framework; it requires engineers and organisations to implement precautions that reflect the heightened risks associated with artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts.

## 8 Case Studies: Real‑World Failures and Their Negligence Implications

Real‑world incidents involving software failures demonstrate how negligence doctrine applies to artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts. These cases illustrate that courts and regulators treat software failures as engineering failures, and that foreseeability, breach, causation, and damage remain intact even when defects arise from opaque or automated systems. Artificial intelligence does not create doctrinal novelty; it amplifies existing duties.

### 8.1 Boeing 737 MAX: MCAS Software Failure

The Boeing 737 MAX crashes, involving Lion Air Flight 610 in 2018 and Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 in 2019, provide a paradigmatic example of software‑driven harm. The Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System relied on a single angle‑of‑attack sensor and repeatedly activated based on erroneous data, forcing the aircraft into a nose‑down attitude. The absence of redundancy, inadequate documentation, and insufficient pilot training contributed to the failures.

The risk of sensor failure was foreseeable. Aviation engineering standards require redundancy and validation of sensor inputs. Failure to implement redundancy, failure to document MCAS behaviour, and failure to train pilots constituted breaches of professional standards. Under *Rogers v Whitaker* [62], professionals must disclose material risks inherent in their tools. MCAS was a factual cause of both crashes under *March v Stramare* [63], and the resulting fatalities constitute damage.

Although MCAS was not artificial intelligence‑generated, its failure mode mirrors artificial intelligence‑generated defects: opaque logic, inadequate documentation, reliance on a single data source, and failure to anticipate foreseeable error.

### 8.2 Knight Capital (2012): Algorithmic Trading Failure

In August 2012, Knight Capital deployed new trading software containing a dormant, untested code path known as “Power Peg”. When activated, it generated millions of erroneous orders within minutes, causing a loss of approximately USD 440 million and destabilising markets.

The risk of deploying untested code was foreseeable. Financial engineering standards require regression testing, sandboxing, and staged deployment. Knight Capital failed to test the new code, remove obsolete logic, implement kill‑switch mechanisms, and follow standard deployment protocols. Under *Wyong Shire Council v Shirt* [64], these were reasonable precautions with minimal burden. The defective code was a factual cause of the loss under *Strong v Woolworths Ltd* [65].

Artificial intelligence‑generated trading strategies exhibit similar risks, including hallucinated logic, non‑deterministic behaviour, opaque decision pathways, and catastrophic amplification of small defects.

### 8.3 Artificial Intelligence‑Enabled Medical Device Recalls

The United States Food and Drug Administration has documented multiple recalls of artificial intelligence‑enabled medical devices due to unsafe algorithmic behaviour. Examples include diagnostic algorithms producing incorrect classifications, dosage calculators generating unsafe recommendations, and autonomous monitoring systems failing to detect critical conditions.

Medical device standards require risk management, validation, and traceability. Artificial intelligence systems with non‑deterministic updates create foreseeable risks. Manufacturers breached duties by failing to validate algorithmic updates, failing to maintain documentation, failing to implement human oversight, and failing to conduct post‑market monitoring. Under *Hill v Van Erp* [66], professionals must anticipate risks inherent in their tools.

These cases demonstrate that artificial intelligence‑generated or artificial intelligence‑modified logic can cause harm across multiple domains, and that negligence doctrine already accommodates such failures.

### 8.4 Summary

These case studies illustrate four principles. First, software failures are engineering failures. Secondly, opacity does not dilute responsibility. Thirdly, artificial intelligence failure modes strengthen foreseeability. Fourthly, governance failures constitute systemic breaches. Negligence doctrine already provides a robust framework for regulating artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts.

## 9 Governance, Compliance, and Role‑Specific Obligations in Artificial Intelligence‑Augmented Engineering

Governance is the mechanism through which engineers and organisations discharge their legal obligations. Artificial intelligence does not change the duty of care; it intensifies it by introducing opacity, unpredictability, and known failure modes. Verification, documentation, oversight, and systemic controls are therefore essential components of negligence‑aligned engineering practice.

### 9.1 Verification and Validation

Verification and validation are core precautions. Under *Shirt* [67], precautions must be taken where risks are foreseeable and the burden of mitigation is reasonable. Artificial intelligence‑generated software satisfies both conditions. Reasonable precautions include mandatory code review, static analysis, dynamic testing, formal methods where appropriate, and prompt‑level review. These controls reflect industry standards such as ISO/IEC 61508, IEC 62304, MISRA C, and the NIST Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework.

### 9.2 Documentation, Provenance, and Traceability

Artificial intelligence systems introduce opacity. Documentation is therefore essential. Under *Rogers v Whitaker* [68], professionals must disclose and manage risks inherent in their tools. Documentation enables risk management, regulatory compliance, and litigation defence. Organisations must maintain prompt logs, model version records, training data provenance where available, review and approval trails, deployment configuration histories, and post‑deployment monitoring logs.

### 9.3 Organisational Controls

Under *Burnie Port Authority v General Jones* [69], entities controlling hazardous operations owe non‑delegable duties. Artificial intelligence‑augmented engineering environments fall within this category. Essential organisational controls include artificial intelligence governance policies, mandatory training, separation of duties, audit trails, risk classification frameworks, kill‑switch mechanisms, and post‑market monitoring.

### 9.4 Role‑Specific Obligations: Engineers

Engineers owe duties grounded in *Rogers v Whitaker* and *Hill v Van Erp*. They must treat artificial intelligence as an untrusted junior engineer, review all artificial intelligence‑generated code, validate artificial intelligence‑generated decisions, document verification steps, understand model limitations, maintain human oversight, escalate anomalies, and refuse unsafe deployment conditions.

### 9.5 Role‑Specific Obligations: Organisations

Organisations owe systemic duties independent of individual engineers. They must implement governance frameworks, enforce verification requirements, provide training, maintain documentation and audit trails, classify artificial intelligence systems by risk, ensure regulatory compliance, allocate resources for safe deployment, and prevent unsafe incentives.

### 9.6 Role‑Specific Obligations: Regulators

Regulators must clarify duties around artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts, require provenance and traceability for artificial intelligence systems, mandate verification and oversight, align artificial intelligence obligations with existing engineering standards, harmonise common‑law and civil‑law approaches, and enforce compliance through audits and penalties.

### 9.7 Divergence Between Negligence and the EU AI Act’s Risk‑Tiered Framework

The European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act is frequently invoked as evidence that artificial intelligence governance aligns with negligence doctrine. This is only partially correct. The Act’s structure diverges from negligence in important ways.

Negligence imposes a unitary standard: reasonable precautions in the circumstances. The standard does not vary by category of system. The Act, by contrast, imposes risk‑tiered obligations:
*   prohibited-risk systems
*   high-risk systems
*   limited-risk systems
*   minimal-risk systems

These categories impose different obligations, including conformity assessments, documentation requirements, logging duties, and human oversight mandates. High-risk systems under the Act correspond to contexts where negligence already demands heightened precautions. However, the Act’s prohibited‑risk category has no negligence analogue, because negligence does not prohibit activities; it regulates them. Conversely, limited‑risk and minimal‑risk systems may still attract negligence liability if harm is foreseeable.

This divergence matters for cross‑jurisdictional analysis. The Act codifies governance obligations, whereas negligence evaluates conduct according to reasonableness. The frameworks converge on foreseeability, documentation, and oversight, but diverge on categorisation, prohibition, and regulatory formality.

## 10 Recommendations

The analysis presented in this paper demonstrates that artificial intelligence intensifies, rather than diminishes, the duty of care owed by engineers and organisations. Artificial intelligence systems introduce opacity, unpredictability, and known failure modes that increase foreseeability and expand the burden of reasonable precautions. The following recommendations synthesise doctrinal, technical, and governance considerations and are structured according to the role‑specific obligations of engineers, organisations, and regulators.

### 10.1 Recommendations for Engineers

Engineers occupy a central position in artificial intelligence‑augmented engineering environments. Their professional obligations derive from *Rogers v Whitaker* [70], *Hill v Van Erp* [71], and *Imbree v McNeilly* [72]. Engineers must adopt practices that reflect the heightened risks associated with artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts.

*   Engineers should treat artificial intelligence systems as untrusted junior engineers.
*   Artificial intelligence outputs require verification, testing, and documentation.
*   Engineers should assume that artificial intelligence systems may hallucinate, fabricate dependencies, or generate insecure patterns.
*   Engineers should maintain human oversight in all safety‑critical contexts. Human‑in‑the‑loop review is a low‑burden precaution that mitigates high‑magnitude risks.
*   Engineers should document verification steps, including prompt logs, model versions, and review outcomes.
*   Documentation supports risk management, regulatory compliance, and litigation defence.
*   Engineers should refuse unsafe deployment conditions.

These recommendations reflect the professional standard of care and align with industry norms, including ISO/IEC 61508, IEC 62304, and MISRA C.

### 10.2 Recommendations for Organisations

Organisations owe systemic duties independent of individual engineers. Under *Burnie Port Authority v General Jones* [73], entities controlling hazardous operations must implement systems that prevent harm. Artificial intelligence‑augmented engineering environments fall within this category. Organisational negligence arises where systemic controls are absent or inadequate.

*   Organisations should implement artificial intelligence governance frameworks that define acceptable use, verification requirements, and risk tiers.
*   These frameworks should incorporate mandatory training on artificial intelligence limitations, legal obligations, and verification practices.
*   Organisations should enforce separation of duties between code generation, review, and deployment.
*   Audit trails should be maintained for all artificial intelligence involvement, including prompt logs, model versions, and review outcomes.
*   Organisations should classify artificial intelligence systems according to risk, drawing on the European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act. High‑risk systems should be subject to heightened oversight, documentation, and testing requirements.
*   Organisations should allocate resources for safe deployment, including time for verification, tools for static and dynamic analysis, and mechanisms for post‑market monitoring.
*   Unsafe incentives, such as prioritising velocity over diligence, should be eliminated.

### 10.3 Recommendations for Regulators

Regulators play a critical role in clarifying duties, harmonising legal frameworks, and enforcing compliance.

*   Regulators should clarify duties around artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts, including obligations relating to verification, documentation, and oversight.
*   Regulators should require provenance and traceability for artificial intelligence systems, including prompt logs, model version records, and deployment histories.
*   Regulators should mandate verification and oversight for high‑risk artificial intelligence systems, drawing on the European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act.
*   Regulators should align artificial intelligence obligations with existing engineering standards, including ISO/IEC 61508, IEC 62304, and MISRA C.
*   Regulators should harmonise common‑law and civil‑law approaches to artificial intelligence governance, ensuring consistency across jurisdictions.
*   Regulators should enforce compliance through audits, penalties, and post‑market monitoring requirements.

### 10.4 Summary

These recommendations reflect doctrinal, technical, and governance considerations. Artificial intelligence intensifies the duty of care by increasing foreseeability, expanding the burden of reasonable precautions, and heightening the significance of organisational governance. Engineers, organisations, and regulators must implement verification, documentation, oversight, and systemic controls that reflect the heightened risks associated with artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts.

## 11 Equitable Principles Potentially Engaged by Artificial Intelligence‑Generated Software

Although negligence provides the primary doctrinal framework for assessing liability arising from artificial intelligence‑generated software, several equitable doctrines may also be engaged. These doctrines operate where relationships of trust, vulnerability, or reliance arise, or where strict common‑law reasoning produces outcomes inconsistent with conscience. Artificial intelligence systems, particularly those embedded in safety‑critical or high‑reliance environments, can create conditions in which equitable intervention is appropriate.

### 11.1 Fiduciary Duties

Fiduciary duties arise where one party undertakes to act in the interests of another, creating a relationship of trust and confidence. Australian courts adopt a functional approach, focusing on vulnerability, reliance, and discretionary power. In *Hospital Products Ltd v United States Surgical Corporation* [74], the High Court emphasised that fiduciary duties arise where one party is entrusted with power affecting the interests of another.

Artificial intelligence‑augmented engineering may engage fiduciary obligations in contexts such as medical devices, financial advice, and professional engineering services. Where engineers or organisations occupy positions of trust, failure to verify artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts may constitute breach of fiduciary duty.

### 11.2 Equitable Estoppel

Equitable estoppel prevents a party from departing from a representation on which another has reasonably relied. In *Waltons Stores (Interstate) Ltd v Maher* [75], the High Court held that estoppel arises where a party induces reliance and then seeks to act inconsistently with that inducement.

Artificial intelligence systems frequently generate outputs accompanied by vendor assurances of reliability or safety. Engineers and organisations may rely on these representations when deploying artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts. If vendors later deny responsibility for defects, equitable estoppel may prevent them from avoiding liability.

### 11.3 Unconscionability

Equitable unconscionability arises where one party exploits the special disadvantage of another. In *Commercial Bank of Australia Ltd v Amadio* [76], the High Court held that unconscionability requires both special disadvantage and exploitation.

Artificial intelligence systems can create informational asymmetries between vendors and users. Vendors possess knowledge of model limitations, training data deficiencies, and failure modes that users cannot access. If vendors exploit this informational disadvantage by overstating reliability or concealing risks, equitable unconscionability may arise.

### 11.4 Breach of Confidence

Artificial intelligence systems often rely on sensitive data, including personal information, proprietary datasets, and confidential engineering artefacts. Breach of confidence arises where information is disclosed or used in a manner inconsistent with an obligation of confidence. In *Coco v AN Clark (Engineers) Ltd* [77], breach of confidence requires confidential information, an obligation of confidence, and unauthorised use.

Artificial intelligence systems may inadvertently disclose or misuse confidential information through training data leakage, prompt‑based disclosure, insecure model outputs, or inadequate access controls.

### 11.5 Equitable Remedies

Equity provides remedies that complement common‑law damages. These include injunctions restraining unsafe deployment of artificial intelligence systems, account of profits where artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts are used to exploit confidential information, constructive trusts where artificial intelligence systems misappropriate proprietary data, and specific performance requiring organisations to implement governance frameworks.

### 11.6 Summary

Equitable principles supplement negligence doctrine in artificial intelligence‑augmented engineering. Fiduciary duties arise where engineers or organisations occupy positions of trust. Estoppel prevents parties from denying representations about artificial intelligence reliability. Unconscionability addresses informational asymmetries and exploitation. Breach of confidence protects sensitive data used by artificial intelligence systems. Equitable remedies provide flexible tools for addressing harm that negligence alone cannot resolve.

### 11.7 Interaction Between Negligence and Equity in Practice

Equitable doctrines do not displace negligence; they operate concurrently. Plaintiffs frequently plead negligence and equitable claims in the alternative, particularly where informational asymmetry, reliance, or vulnerability is present. Equitable remedies, including injunctions and account of profits, may supplement or replace common‑law damages. Contributory negligence does not ordinarily apply to equitable wrongs, creating strategic implications for plaintiffs. Fiduciary duties may impose stricter obligations than negligence, particularly where discretionary power and vulnerability coincide. Estoppel may prevent defendants from denying responsibility even where negligence analysis is contested. Equity therefore interacts with negligence by providing additional pathways to liability and additional remedial flexibility.

## 12 Conclusion

Artificial intelligence systems capable of generating software artefacts have become embedded within contemporary engineering practice. Their adoption has accelerated the delegation of core cognitive tasks to opaque, stochastic models, thereby reshaping professional norms and expanding the risk landscape. This paper has argued that artificial intelligence intensifies, rather than diminishes, the duty of care owed by engineers and organisations. Negligence doctrine evaluates conduct according to the standard of the reasonable professional, and artificial intelligence does not alter this analytical structure. Instead, artificial intelligence amplifies foreseeability, increases the burden of reasonable precautions, and heightens the significance of organisational governance.

The analysis has demonstrated that existing legal principles are sufficient to regulate artificial intelligence‑augmented engineering. The duty of care requires engineers and organisations to implement verification, documentation, and oversight mechanisms that reflect the heightened risks associated with artificial intelligence. Breach arises where engineers fail to inspect artificial intelligence‑generated code, fail to understand model limitations, fail to maintain human oversight, or fail to implement governance frameworks. Causation remains intact, even where artificial intelligence introduces opaque decision pathways or multi‑actor causal chains. Damage encompasses physical harm, economic loss, and regulatory consequences.

Real‑world case studies, including the Boeing MCAS failures, the Knight Capital trading incident, and recalls of artificial intelligence‑enabled medical devices, illustrate that artificial intelligence‑generated or artificial intelligence‑modified logic can cause harm across multiple domains. These cases demonstrate that negligence doctrine already accommodates artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts. Civil‑law systems, including those of Germany, France, Japan, and Korea, converge with common‑law principles on foreseeability, preventability, and objective fault. The European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act further codifies governance obligations that align with the reasonable engineer standard, although its risk‑tiered structure diverges from negligence’s unitary standard.

Equitable principles supplement this analysis by addressing relationships of trust, reliance, vulnerability, and conscience. Fiduciary duties, estoppel, unconscionability, and breach of confidence provide additional mechanisms for regulating artificial intelligence‑augmented engineering, particularly in contexts involving informational asymmetry or reliance on representations. These doctrines reinforce the conclusion that artificial intelligence intensifies responsibility.

Artificial intelligence does not require a new liability regime. Instead, it requires engineers and organisations to implement governance systems that reflect the heightened risks associated with artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts. Negligence doctrine and equitable principles together provide a robust framework for regulating artificial intelligence‑augmented engineering. The future of engineering practice therefore depends on the integration of technical competence, legal literacy, and governance maturity.

## 13 Counter‑Arguments and Rebuttals

A complete analysis requires engagement with the opposing view: that existing doctrine is insufficient and that artificial intelligence requires new liability categories. Several scholars argue that artificial intelligence introduces epistemic opacity, autonomous behaviour, and distributed responsibility that cannot be addressed through traditional negligence frameworks. This section outlines the principal counter‑arguments and provides doctrinal rebuttals grounded in Australian law, comparative civil‑law principles, and engineering governance.

### 13.1 Counter‑Argument: Artificial Intelligence Is Autonomous and Therefore Requires Strict Liability

Some scholars argue that artificial intelligence systems exhibit autonomous behaviour that cannot be traced to human decisions, thereby requiring strict liability. This argument rests on the premise that artificial intelligence systems act independently of human control.

**Rebuttal:** Artificial intelligence systems are tools. Tools do not act independently of those who design, deploy, or oversee them. Under *Fennell v Robson Excavations* [78], delegation does not discharge duty. Under *Kondis v State Transport Authority* [79], duties in hazardous contexts are non‑delegable. Artificial intelligence behaviour is attributable to those who deploy it. Strict liability is unnecessary because negligence already captures failures to verify, test, document, and oversee artificial intelligence systems.

### 13.2 Counter‑Argument: Artificial Intelligence Creates Epistemic Opacity That Defeats Negligence Analysis

This argument holds that artificial intelligence systems are too opaque for engineers to understand, making it impossible to determine breach or causation.

**Rebuttal:** Opacity strengthens foreseeability. Under *Wyong Shire Council v Shirt* [80], foreseeable risks require precautions. Artificial intelligence opacity is well‑documented. Engineers know, or ought to know, that artificial intelligence systems hallucinate, fabricate dependencies, and behave non‑deterministically. These characteristics increase the burden of reasonable precautions. Causation remains intact under *March v Stramare* [81] because artificial intelligence behaviour is not extraordinary, unforeseeable, or independent of human conduct.

### 13.3 Counter‑Argument: Artificial Intelligence Requires Model‑Specific Duties

Some scholars propose new duties tailored to artificial intelligence, including duties to audit models, monitor drift, or ensure explainability.

**Rebuttal:** These duties already exist within negligence. Under *Rogers v Whitaker* [82], professionals must exercise independent judgment and adopt precautions that reflect contemporary knowledge. Model auditing, drift monitoring, and explainability are reasonable precautions where risks are foreseeable. Negligence doctrine is flexible enough to incorporate new technical practices without creating new liability categories.

### 13.4 Counter‑Argument: Artificial Intelligence Requires Statutory Safe Harbours

Industry advocates propose safe harbours for artificial intelligence developers, arguing that liability will stifle innovation.

**Rebuttal:** Safe harbours weaken incentives for diligence. Negligence doctrine already balances innovation and safety by imposing reasonable, not absolute, precautions. Under *Hill v Van Erp* [83], professionals must anticipate risks inherent in their tools. Artificial intelligence systems introduce foreseeable risks that require oversight. Safe harbours would undermine public safety and are unnecessary because negligence already accommodates technological evolution.

### 13.5 Counter‑Argument: Artificial Intelligence Requires Autonomous System Liability

Some scholars propose treating artificial intelligence systems as legal persons capable of bearing liability.

**Rebuttal:** This approach lacks doctrinal foundation. Liability attaches to persons who owe duties, not to tools. Artificial intelligence systems cannot owe duties because they cannot exercise judgment, understand obligations, or bear consequences. Under Australian law, liability attaches to engineers, organisations, and regulators. Artificial intelligence systems are instruments, not actors.

### 13.6 Summary

These counter‑arguments reflect legitimate concerns about artificial intelligence opacity, unpredictability, and distributed responsibility. However, negligence doctrine already accommodates these characteristics. Artificial intelligence intensifies responsibility by expanding foreseeability and increasing the burden of reasonable precautions. Equity supplements negligence by addressing informational asymmetry, reliance, and vulnerability. Together, negligence and equity provide a robust framework for regulating artificial intelligence‑generated artefacts. New liability categories are unnecessary.
