## **Abstract**

Governed computational ecosystems are socio‑technical systems designed to maintain coherence, lineage, and conceptual integrity across time. They differ from mainstream software ecosystems not in degree but in kind. Governed ecosystems embed structural invariants, provenance tracking, long‑arc alignment, governed integration, and star‑agnostic boundary maintenance as constitutive commitments. These commitments function as regulatory constraints, memory substrates, temporal stabilisers, coupling mechanisms, and autonomy filters.

Drawing on cybernetics, systems theory, philosophy of technology, information ethics, and software architecture, this monograph argues that governed ecosystems constitute self‑regulating technological forms of life. Through detailed case studies - anchored by AusRewrite‑T5, a hybrid symbolic–neural legal rewriting engine - the monograph demonstrates how governed ecosystems preserve intelligibility across symbolic, neural, and hybrid computational domains. The implications are epistemic, architectural, organisational, and philosophical. Governed ecosystems provide the conditions under which long‑arc research programmes can remain stable, intelligible, and conceptually coherent.

## **Introduction**

Mainstream software ecosystems prioritise convenience, visibility, and rapid iteration. These environments lack the structural, epistemic, and temporal commitments required for long‑arc research. As a consequence, research artefacts frequently drift, fragment, or lose interpretability over time.

This monograph introduces governed computational ecosystems as a conceptual and architectural response. Governed ecosystems maintain coherence through explicit regulatory structures, systemic attractors, and epistemic commitments. The monograph is designed for GitHub distribution and integrates directly with repository artefacts, including architectural specifications, constraints, schemas, and version histories.

The argument proceeds through background, definition, architecture, case studies, comparison, implications, and conclusion. AusRewrite‑T5 serves as the primary evidential case, demonstrating the operational reality of governed ecosystems.

## **Background**

Cybernetics provides the vocabulary of regulation, feedback, and system memory. Wiener emphasises feedback loops.[1] Ashby’s theory of requisite variety highlights the importance of constraints.[2] Von Foerster’s second‑order cybernetics introduces reflexivity: systems must regulate not only their operations but also their conditions of regulation.[3]

Systems theory contributes structural coupling, boundary maintenance, and attractor states. Bertalanffy emphasises relational complexity.[4] Luhmann highlights boundary maintenance as a condition of autonomy.[5] Maturana and Varela introduce autopoiesis: systems maintain identity through internal processes.[6]

Philosophy of technology provides ontological and epistemic framing. Simondon emphasises technical individuation.[7] Stiegler’s account of technics and temporality introduces tertiary retention - technical memory.[8] Winner demonstrates that artefacts embody governance choices.[9]

Information ethics contributes epistemic integrity. Floridi argues that information systems possess ontological structure, epistemic commitments, and ethical implications.[10]

Software architecture provides engineering foundations. Parnas emphasises modularity.[11] Brooks emphasises conceptual integrity.[12] Shaw and Garlan provide architectural styles for stability and intelligibility.[13]

Together, these traditions provide the conceptual scaffolding for governed computational ecosystems.

## **Formal Definition**

Governed computational ecosystems are socio‑technical systems that maintain coherence, intelligibility, and lineage through explicit regulatory structures. They differ from mainstream ecosystems not in degree but in kind. A governed ecosystem is defined by five constitutive commitments:

- **structural invariants**  
- **provenance tracking**  
- **long‑arc alignment**  
- **governed integration**  
- **star‑agnostic boundary maintenance**

Structural invariants function as constitutive grammar. Provenance provides system memory. Long‑arc alignment introduces teleological direction. Governed integration ensures coherent interaction under shared constraints. Star‑agnostic boundary maintenance filters external social signals from governance inputs.

Together, these commitments define governed ecosystems as self‑regulating technological forms of life.

## **Architectural Model**

Governed ecosystems maintain coherence through six architectural principles:

- project‑first architecture  
- package‑heavy composition  
- star‑agnostic sovereignty  
- unified asset registry  
- shared invariants  
- operator‑tier alignment  

The unified asset registry functions as a system of tertiary retention in Stiegler’s sense: a technical memory substrate that records the temporal evolution of artefacts and preserves lineage, identity, and architectural commitments across time.

## **Case Studies**

### **AusRewrite‑T5 (Primary Evidential Case)**  
AusRewrite‑T5 is a hybrid symbolic–neural rewriting engine designed to convert any English text into strict Australian English with a formal legal register. It integrates:

- regex pre‑processing  
- Prolog rule‑based constraints  
- LLM semantic rewriting under symbolic governance  

#### **Provenance as Explicit System Memory**  
AusRewrite‑T5 uses Git‑style provenance. Every rewrite is a commit‑like event recorded in an append‑only, immutable structure. Provenance includes:

- input text  
- regex lineage  
- Prolog rule applications  
- LLM rewrite metadata  
- post‑validation results  
- semantic fidelity notes  
- version snapshots  

This explicit temporal structure provides the system’s memory substrate.

#### **Structural Invariants Across Three Layers**  
AusRewrite‑T5 enforces invariants at three levels:

1. **Schema‑level invariants**  
2. **Prolog‑level invariants**  
3. **Pipeline‑level invariants**  

This multi‑layer enforcement ensures coherence across representational regimes.

#### **Adversarial Failure Case: Citation Pattern Violation**  
A critical test occurred when the LLM rewrote a sentence containing a case citation. The neural component preserved meaning but altered the pinpoint reference. The Prolog validator rejected the output because the citation no longer matched the required AGLC‑adjacent pattern.

In a mainstream ecosystem, the error passes silently.  
In a governed ecosystem, it is caught, logged, and corrected.  
This is not a difference of degree but of constitutive structure.

#### **Semantic Fidelity Under Constraint**  
Semantic fidelity is validated through Prolog predicates that check modal verbs, legal terminology, and clause structure. Meaning is preserved; register and style change.

## **Comparison with Mainstream Ecosystems**

Mainstream ecosystems rely on conventions; governed ecosystems rely on constraints. Mainstream ecosystems treat artefacts as outputs; governed ecosystems treat artefacts as lineage‑bearing entities. Mainstream ecosystems optimise for short‑arc iteration; governed ecosystems optimise for long‑arc alignment.

The AusRewrite‑T5 citation failure case illustrates the categorical difference. In a mainstream ecosystem, the error passes silently. In a governed ecosystem, it is caught, logged, and corrected. This is not a difference of degree but of constitutive structure.

## **Implications**

### **Epistemic Implications**  
Governed ecosystems treat knowledge as a lineage‑bearing process rather than a collection of artefacts. Provenance becomes a condition of intelligibility; without lineage, information cannot be situated or trusted. Floridi’s philosophy of information reinforces this view by emphasising that information systems possess ontological structure and epistemic commitments.[10] Structural invariants reduce ambiguity by ensuring interpretability across temporal scales. Long‑arc alignment prevents conceptual drift, enabling multi‑year research programmes to maintain direction.

### **Architectural Implications**  
Governed ecosystems redefine architecture as a regime of regulation rather than an arrangement of components. Structural invariants become constitutive constraints that define what can exist within the system. Packages become technical individuals with lineage and systemic roles. Projects become regulated subsystems with identity. The unified asset registry becomes the system’s memory substrate. Operator‑tier alignment becomes a systemic attractor. These commitments transform computational environments into systems with identity, memory, boundaries, and teleology.

### **Organisational Implications**  
Governance becomes a technical activity rather than an administrative one. Governed ecosystems prioritise custodial stewardship over open contribution, regulated integration over federation, and architectural sovereignty over popularity‑driven direction. Star‑agnostic boundary maintenance filters external social signals from governance inputs, preserving epistemic autonomy. These commitments require custodial institutions capable of maintaining structural invariants, provenance, and long‑arc alignment.

### **Philosophical Implications**  
Governed ecosystems challenge existing assumptions about what computational systems are and what they can become. They constitute technological forms of life with identity, memory, boundaries, and teleology. They demonstrate that technical artefacts possess lineage, conceptual integrity, and systemic roles. They show that governance is not an external imposition but a constitutive feature of technical systems. These commitments provide a philosophical foundation for understanding governed ecosystems as regulated socio‑technical systems capable of supporting long‑arc research.

## **Conclusion**

Governed computational ecosystems represent a shift in how computational environments are understood, designed, and inhabited. They provide the conditions under which long‑arc research programmes can remain stable, intelligible, and conceptually coherent. AusRewrite‑T5 demonstrates that governed ecosystems are not abstractions but operational systems with regulatory structures, systemic attractors, and epistemic commitments.
